# White paper plan: the economic outlook for Austin's working musicians

**Drafted** 2026-08-01 for Eric Booth's review. Nothing in this plan has been
analyzed or written yet. The evidence round is complete for ten of eleven
workstreams; the cost-of-living pull is being re-run after the first attempt
hung.

**Research question.** How friendly are Texas and Austin policies, programs, and
economic opportunities to working musicians in Austin, measured against other
gig workers and creative occupations in the Texas economy?

---

## 1. What the evidence will and will not let us say

One finding from the evidence round changes the shape of the paper, so it
belongs at the front of the plan rather than in a limitations section.

**No one has measured what an Austin musician earns since 2013.** The 2014
Austin Music Census (fielded 2013, published 2015 by Titan Music Group) asked
income questions and found that 68.4% of respondents earned under $10,000 from
music. The 2022 Greater Austin Music Census deliberately removed the income
questions because, in its own account, they turned off respondents. No Austin
census has followed 2022. Every musician-income figure circulating in Austin
journalism in 2026 traces back to that 2013 fieldwork, and one widely repeated
number, $15,475, turns out to be a single saxophonist's 1981 salary recovered
from a bandleader's calendar.

That gap constrains the paper in a productive way. We cannot report a current
Austin musician income from a local survey, and the paper will not imply we can. What
we can do is triangulate from federal microdata that does still measure these
workers, state and city administrative records, and an original venue-revenue
panel, then say plainly where the numbers come from and how far they stretch.
The measurement gap is itself one of the paper's findings: a city that describes
itself as the Live Music Capital of the World stopped counting what its
musicians earn.

A second constraint shapes every employment number. The two federal series that
report music jobs and wages, the Occupational Employment and Wage Statistics
survey and the Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages, cover payroll jobs
only. They exclude the self-employed, who are 44% of Texas musicians. Read
alone, those series describe a shrinking, better-paid occupation. Read against
the microdata and the nonemployer counts, they describe work moving out of
payroll and into an economically weaker gig layer that the headline series
cannot see. Getting that interpretation right is the analytic core of the paper.

## 2. The argument

**Thesis.** Austin and Texas have built real institutions for music, and those
institutions are small, volatile, and pointed at venues, tourism, and real
estate rather than at musician earnings. The state subsidizes the music business
through the alcohol its audiences buy and gives film fifteen times what it gives
music. The city banks a fixed slice of hotel tax for musicians, then disburses
it unevenly, skips a year, and has never audited it. Meanwhile the stage itself
is contracting: core live-music rooms took in 18.6% less in real terms in 2025
than in 2019, while Austin's bar market grew and its bar count rose 27.8%. The
honest answer to "how friendly?" is that Austin is friendlier to music as an
amenity than to musicians as workers, and no one is measuring the difference.

**Structure.** Seven short sections, each stating its finding in the heading.

1. *Austin stopped counting what its musicians earn.* The measurement gap, what
   replaced it, and how the paper triangulates. Sets up method honestly and
   early. (~1.5 pp, Figure 1)
2. *A working musician in Texas earns about half what the median worker earns.*
   The microdata spine: median personal income near $22,800 against a $45,000
   baseline, 44% self-employment against 9.8%, 25 median hours. National
   benchmarks agree: two credible national surveys report medians of $18,000
   and $21,300, and Charlotte's 2019 census independently echoes Austin's 2013
   result. (~2 pp, Figures 2 and 3)
3. *The payroll job is disappearing; the gig is not.* Payroll musician jobs fell
   28% nationally and roughly halved in Texas over two decades while wages rose,
   and independent-artist nonemployer businesses grew 62% in Travis County.
   Same workers, different legal wrapper, weaker economics. (~1.5 pp, Figure 4)
4. *The stage is shrinking while the bar market grows.* The original venue
   panel: 114 venues, 234 months, deflated. Core rooms down 18.6% from 2019 while
   citywide receipts rose 2.8% and permits rose 27.8%; outdoor music venue
   permits down 31.9% from peak; Red River down 22.1%. Closure causes from the
   news record put rent and property transactions ahead of COVID. (~2.5 pp,
   Figures 5 and 6)
5. *Austin's music money is small, late, and unaudited.* The Live Music Fund at
   2.9% of hotel tax against historic preservation's 4.4 times larger share;
   $5.13M banked unspent through the worst of the pandemic; average awards
   swinging from $9,458 to $32,096 to $17,895 with a skipped year in between;
   13 cents of every dollar spent on administration and advertising; no audit
   ever performed, and the one adjacent audit excluded music on purpose.
   (~2.5 pp, Figures 7 and 8)
6. *Texas funds the music business, not the musician, and funds film far more.*
   The Music Incubator Rebate scales with alcohol tax remitted and sets no
   artist-compensation floor; $300M per biennium goes to moving images against
   $20.2M to music from the same office. Per-capita disbursement benchmarking
   against New York, Louisiana, Tennessee, and Georgia, where a $15M annual cap
   has paid out zero for five years. Texas's arts sector is 2.5% of state GDP,
   the smallest share among eight comparison states. (~2.5 pp, Figures 9 and 10)
7. *What would change the answer.* Options framed as "consider," tied to the
   specific evidence gap each one closes: restore earnings measurement; attach
   artist-pay conditions to venue subsidy; audit the fund and stabilize the
   award cycle; treat venue capital access as distinct from rent relief;
   benchmark on dollars disbursed rather than caps authorized. (~1.5 pp)

The draft also carves out two boxed sidebars, each roughly a third of a page,
placed inside sections 3 and 6:

- *Apprenticeship.* "Musician" is a federally apprenticeable occupation (RAPIDS
  2080CB), and no active registered program appears to exist anywhere in the
  country. Sixteen of 1,440 apprenticeable titles are arts-adjacent, and they
  are crew and technical roles. The model needs an employer-sponsor, which fits
  a venue's production crew and not a freelance performer. This answers the
  question in Eric's notes directly.
- *Streaming.* Streaming supplies 1.5% of musician income at a median of $100 a
  year, against 28-42% from live performance and 12-22% from teaching. The
  royalty argument, whatever its merits, is not where an Austin musician's
  income problem lives. Every advocacy per-stream figure in circulation traces
  to one unaudited blog, so the sidebar states ranges and sources rather than a
  point estimate.

**Page budget.** Body sections total roughly 14 pages including figures, which
leaves no room for references and a methods appendix inside a 15-page limit. See
the open decisions in section 8.

## 4. Analysis plan

Stata for the microdata and panel work, matching team conventions; the venue
panel is already assembled and deflated.

**Microdata (PUMS).** Apply ADJINC to all dollar amounts before any comparison;
the collection-round figures are nominal and not yet adjusted. Use PWGTP for
point estimates and the PWGTP1-80 replicate weights for standard errors, since
Austin-only musician cells are thin (129 unweighted records). Join the housing
file on SERIALNO, verified as a complete match, to produce rent-burden shares
for musician households against the all-worker baseline. Report Austin MSA
figures with explicit margins of error, and suppress any cell too thin to
support publication. Occupation codes are verified: 2751 is Music Directors and
Composers, 2752 is Musicians and Singers, and no clean sound-engineer code
exists.

**Substitution estimate (Figure 4).** Combine payroll jobs from OEWS, UI-covered
jobs from QCEW, and nonemployer establishments from NES to describe the changing
composition of music work. State the arithmetic openly and do not present the
sum as a headcount: nonemployer establishments count businesses rather than
people, and one person can hold both a payroll job and a nonemployer business.
This is a composition comparison, not a workforce census.

**Venue panel.** Already deflated to June 2026 dollars with the missing October
2025 CPI interpolated and flagged. Use the `_ex_festival` columns throughout to
exclude the ten festival-aggregation rows. Report the balanced panel alongside
the full panel so the reader can see the composition effect, and keep the
large-ticketed-venue exclusion explicit, since it is what separates an apparent
recovery from the core-tier decline.

**Deflation and per-capita conventions.** One deflator for the whole paper,
stated once: CPI-U, June 2026 dollars. Per-capita denominators use Census
Vintage 2025 population, July 1, 2025. Any figure mixing nominal and real
series gets a note in the caption.

**City fund figures.** Use the city's stated headline totals rather than
line-level tallies, because the FY2026 award list does not reconcile with itself
(a $90,000 gap, and 21 venues named against a subtotal of 20). Flag the
discrepancy in a footnote. The cumulative fund-balance figure is a cash-flow
proxy that does not reconcile with press reporting; either obtain the audited
schedule or drop the balance claim and report revenue against expenditure by
year instead.

## 6. Claims we will not make

The evidence round surfaced several widely repeated numbers that do not survive
checking. Recording them here keeps them out of the draft.

- *"90% of Austin venues will close."* Two conditional projections conflated,
  both premised on relief that then arrived. Austin's actual 2020 closure cohort
  was roughly 11 named venues.
- *"$150 a gig, unchanged since the 1980s."* This merges a per-gig market rate
  with the city's own per-hour booking rate; the units differ. The documented
  city rate series ($100/hour in 2012, $150/hour in 2016, $200 per musician in
  2023) is usable and interesting on its own.
- *"$15,475 median musician income."* One saxophonist's 1981 salary.
- *Any Texas Music Office directory count.* No verifiable figure is published;
  the numbers in circulation trace to pages that now return errors.
- *Citigroup's "artists get 12%."* Cited backwards in advocacy materials; the
  Citigroup figure describes a rise from 7%, and the denominator includes
  advertising revenue.
- *A Texas share of streaming royalties.* No source supports one. The sidebar
  will present national figures and say so.
- *Per-stream payout point estimates.* All advocacy figures trace to a single
  unaudited blog. Present ranges with sources named.

Four items need verification before publication rather than exclusion: the
University of Texas at Austin's $10M shuttered-venue award, coded as a live
venue operator; the final status of the Agent of Change ordinance; the Live
Music Fund's audited balance; and the Red River district's economic-impact
figures, which come from an advocacy study built on a 2021 trough.

## 7. What is still missing

Four gaps would require a public information request, and each takes weeks:

1. The Texas Music Incubator Rebate recipient list. The appropriations act
   requires the state to report film-incentive recipients by name and amount,
   and imposes no parallel requirement for music, so no list is published.
2. Live Music Fund applicant demographics and genre breakdowns, currently
   visible only inside dashboards with no export path.
3. The audited Live Music Fund balance schedule.
4. Music and Entertainment division budget and headcount before FY2026, which
   requires the printed budget volumes and is complicated by the February 2025
   reorganization.

Two smaller items need only a phone call: the American Federation of Musicians
Local 433 scale for Austin, which is unpublished, and whether Sound Music Cities
retains unpublished Austin data from its 2023-24 work.

## 8. Open decisions for Eric

1. **Page budget.** Seven sections with ten or eleven figures runs to about 14
   pages before references and methods. Options: hold to 15 pages total and cut
   sections 3 and 7 to half a page each; allow a methods appendix outside the
   15-page count; or split into a 12-page brief plus a separate technical
   appendix.
2. **Whether to file the public information requests now.** Filing delays
   publication by roughly four to six weeks. Publishing without them means
   documenting the gaps as findings, which is defensible and arguably makes the
   accountability point more sharply.
3. **Audience and framing.** A brief aimed at the 90th Legislature emphasizes
   the interstate benchmark and the film comparison. One aimed at Austin
   emphasizes the fund's administration and the venue panel. One aimed at
   funders emphasizes musician earnings and the measurement gap. The evidence
   supports all three; the emphasis and the recommendations differ.
4. **Whether to include a policy scorecard.** A summary table rating Austin and
   Texas across direct artist support, venue support, pay standards, health
   access, career pipeline, and measurement would answer the research question
   in one view and give an advocate something usable in a meeting. It also
   invites methodological
   argument about weighting. Recommend including it as a qualitative,
   evidence-anchored table with no composite score.
5. **Comparison geography.** The paper is Austin-centric with a Texas policy
   layer. Adding Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio comparisons is feasible from
   the nonemployer and BEA data and would speak to a statewide audience, at a
   cost of roughly a page.
