# Fact-check: Austin musician income measurement

**Prepared:** 2026-08-02
**Scope:** Five claims proposed for a white paper on Austin musician economics.
**Method:** Primary sources on disk first, verified independently rather than taken from the folder's prior synthesis notes. Image-only PDF pages were rendered and read visually. Web retrieval used only to close specific gaps, and each use is noted.

**Page-number convention.** Both Austin censuses are cited two ways where they differ: `printed p.N` is the number shown on the page; `PDF p.N` is the position in the file. For the 2015 census, printed = PDF − 2.

---

## Summary of verdicts

| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| 1. The 2022 census deliberately dropped income questions because they turned off respondents | **Supported in the primary document, with two wording corrections** |
| 2. "No one has measured what an Austin musician earns since 2013" | **Not defensible as written — the City of Austin itself published a musician wage for 2016 and 2017** |
| 3. Federal series tracking musician earnings in Austin/Texas | **Substantially confirmed, with three corrections** |
| 4. $15,475 = one saxophonist's 1981 salary from a bandleader's calendar | **Verified at source, verbatim** |
| 5. 2026 Austin journalism's income figures trace to 2013 fieldwork | **Too strong, and mis-diagnoses the problem. Defensible version below.** |

# CLAIM 1

> "The 2022 Greater Austin Music Census deliberately dropped its income questions because they turned off respondents."

## Verdict: Supported by the primary document itself, not by secondary reporting. Two wording corrections needed.

### Where it comes from

The basis is the census document itself, in its own front matter — not an interview, not a methodology appendix, and not journalism.

**Verbatim quote (23 words):**

> "removed questions from the 2014 survey that aimed to quantify the income of music people in a way that turned off many respondents"

**Citation:** *2022 Greater Austin Music Census — Summary Report*, Sound Music Cities, LLC, published February 2023. Section "A Community Effort," **printed p.2 (PDF p.3)**. Local file: `austin_music_census_2022_SUMMARY_REPORT.pdf`. Source URL: https://www.austinmusiccensus.org/s/2022-Greater-Austin-Music-Census-SUMMARY-REPORT-v13-optimized.pdf

The full sentence, for context:

> "With the guidance of the community, the 2022 survey updated demographic questions to include more diversity, equity, and inclusion elements and removed questions from the 2014 survey that aimed to quantify the income of music people in a way that turned off many respondents."

The following sentence states the rationale: *"This approach garnered more trust and ownership by the community itself."*

**Verification note.** The Summary Report has no text layer. Page 3 of the PDF was rendered at 130 dpi and read visually. The quote is in body text, not a caption or pull-quote.

### Correction 1: it was not a unilateral analyst decision

The sentence opens "With the guidance of the community." The report attributes the removal to its Community Engagement Partners, and pairs it with a trust-building rationale. "Deliberately dropped" is accurate; "deliberately dropped, on the advice of its community partners" is more accurate and is what the document says.

### Correction 2: what was dropped is narrower than "its income questions"

The document says it removed the questions "that aimed to **quantify** the income of music people." The 2022 instrument still asked about income — it stopped asking for dollars. Read strictly, the sentence also says the off-putting thing was the *way* the 2014 questions quantified income, not income questions in general. Both readings are available in the text; the paper should not claim the stronger one ("asking musicians about income turns them off") since the sentence does not clearly say that.

**Recommended formulation:** "On its community partners' advice, the 2022 census removed the 2014 survey's income-quantification questions, which the report says had turned off many respondents."

### Competing explanations in circulation — checked, and only one is documented

Three other rationales circulate. The paper should use none of them.

- **"Because the pandemic disrupted the industry."** This phrasing appears in search-engine summaries of the 2022 census coverage. It could not be traced to any source. The likeliest origin is a **respondent** write-in on Data Appendix p.86 — *"I'm glad you did not ask about income. We've been in a pandemic for 2 1/2 years"* — which is one participant's view, not the census team's stated reason. **Do not attribute a pandemic rationale to Sound Music Cities.**
- **"Because the questions were too sensitive."** From the UT Austin project: *"Even the 2022 Music Census did not report on (or collect) the income of survey participants, apparently considering such questions too sensitive."* Note the word **"apparently"** — the UT author was inferring, not citing. Not usable as a source for intent.
- **Contemporaneous confirmation of the fact, with no reason given.** Austin Chronicle, Rachel Rascoe, July 15, 2022, at the census launch: *"Compared to the 2015 focus on industry dollar amounts, the new census doesn't request any numbers on earnings."* This independently confirms **that** earnings questions were absent from the instrument as fielded, six months before the report was published — useful corroboration, but it offers no explanation. (https://www.austinchronicle.com/daily/music/2022-07-15/for-the-first-time-since-2015-a-new-austin-music-census-is-now-open/)

**The Summary Report sentence quoted above is the only documented statement of why.** Anything else is inference.

### What is separately and independently verifiable

- **The 2022 instrument contains no dollar-denominated income question.** Confirmed by reviewing all 87 slides of `austin_music_census_2022_DATA_APPENDIX.pdf`. No slide reports musician income in dollars. The only dollar figures reported for creatives are **expenses**, not earnings (p.46).
- Respondent reaction is corroborated by two write-ins in the 2022 Data Appendix, **p.86** ("What question do you wish we'd asked?"): *"I'm glad you did not ask about income. We've been in a pandemic for 2 1/2 years."* and, in the opposite direction, *"I wish y'all would have asked how much of income goes towards rent."* These are two individual comments on a sample-of-comments slide, not a measured share. Do not present them as evidence of how common either view was.

## The 2022 census DID ask income-adjacent questions. The paper must not overstate the gap.

All page numbers below are `austin_music_census_2022_DATA_APPENDIX.pdf`, whose printed slide numbers match PDF page numbers. Chart values were read from rendered images.

### 1. Share of income by source — asked, ordinally (pp. 40–41)

**This is the closest thing to an income question in the 2022 instrument, and it is easy to miss.** Slide 40, "Creatives: Sources of Current Music Income," asks music creatives to rate each revenue stream on a five-point scale: **All / Most / Some / Little / None**. Slide 41 footnotes the aggregation explicitly: *"(aggregating responses 'all, most, some, little' excluding 'none')."*

Distribution for local live gigs (p.40): **All 13%, Most 38%, Some 24%, Little 17%, None 8%.** So roughly half of music creatives (13% + 38% = 51%) said local gigs supply all or most of their music income.

Read carefully, this is a *within-music* share question — what fraction of music income comes from each stream — not what fraction of total income comes from music. It yields no dollars. But it is a genuine income question, and "the 2022 census dropped its income questions" without qualification is wrong.

*Caution for reproduction:* on the six non-local-gig rows the "All" segment is either absent or too small to carry a printed label, so the visible segments shift one category left. Cite the local-gigs row, where all five segments are labeled, and avoid reconstructing the others from the chart.

### 2. Second jobs / work outside music — asked (p.15)

Slide 15, "Work Outside of Music (Features)":

| Response | Share |
|---|---|
| Yes, works in a non-creative industry | **38%** |
| No outside work | **32%** |
| Yes, works in another creative industry | 24% |
| Outside job is the "primary" job | **22%** |
| Outside work is creative | 8% |
| Student, retired, otherwise not working | 6% |

Slide callouts: *"One in five consider their other job 'primary'"* and *"A third are full-time in music."* The Summary Report restates the 38% on printed p.10.

### 3. Financial precarity by expense category — asked (p.71)

Slide 71, "Expenses Currently Struggling to Afford," all respondents:

| Expense | Struggling to afford |
|---|---|
| Rent / mortgage | **38%** |
| Dental, vision insurance | 28% |
| Health care | 21% |
| Health insurance | 21% |
| Nutritious food | 17% |
| Transportation | 16% |
| Prescription drugs | 12% |
| Child / elder care | 7% |

### 4. Housing cost stress — asked (pp. 75–77)

- Tenure (p.75/76): 60% renter, 47% homeowner, 3% housed but not paying, **1% no permanent or stable housing** (categories overlap as published). Versus 2014: renting 56.8% → 53.2%, owning 43.2% → 46.8% — ownership slightly **up**, which cuts against a simple displacement story and should be reported.
- Changes over the past two years (p.77): **44%** say housing costs rose; **15%** say housing costs caused a move; 17% moved once locally; 14% more than once.
- Distance from downtown (pp. 6–7): average 10.6–10.7 miles, median 8 miles; 12.9 miles for BIPOC respondents and 12.6 for the uninsured, against 6.6 for venue owners.

### 5. Gig volume and deal structure — asked (pp. 42–45, 58)

- Local paid gigs per month, creatives: **0 gigs 7.6% (2014) → 14% (2022)**; more than 3 gigs **43% → 35%**. Restricting to creatives who said their work had returned to pre-pandemic levels leaves more than 3 gigs at 38%.
- **54% of gigs pay a base guarantee** (p.45); the rest are percentage or door deals. No dollar amount was collected.

### 6. Business expenses — asked in dollars (p.46)

Average annual spend by creatives **$10,500**, of which 60% ($6,300) is local. Components: new music production $3,538, workspace $1,390, gear rentals $1,378, promotion $1,350, merch $921, supplies $865, accounting/legal $585, social media/web $473. **This is the only dollar-denominated economic quantity the 2022 census collected from creatives — and it is a cost, not an income.**

### 7. Economic-status proxies that survive in the DEI crosstabs

The DEI appendix (`austin_music_census_2022_DEI_APPENDIX.pdf`, pp. 9–15) crosstabs attitude items by "No Health Insurance" and "Rent Home." Uninsured respondents are the most negative subgroup on nearly every dimension (76% call the scene elitist vs. 51% of homeowners). These are the closest thing to an income gradient in the 2022 data. Descriptive only; subgroups overlap heavily and no multivariate analysis was published.

### What the 2022 census did NOT ask

Checked directly against the full slide inventory and a keyword scan of the Data Appendix text layer:

- **No dollar income question of any kind** (annual, monthly, or per gig).
- **No emergency-expense or savings question.** There is no "could you cover an unexpected $400 expense" item or equivalent. The only debt-adjacent slide is p.26, "Access to Capital for Music Business Growth" ("Most don't want to take on debt"), which is about financing a music business, not household liquidity.
- **No share-of-total-income-from-music question in percentage or dollar terms.** The nearest proxy is "outside job is primary" (22%, p.15).

### A caution the paper should carry

The 2022 Summary Report, **printed p.10**, headlines Insight 4: *"While incomes are diversifying and improving for Austin's music creatives…"* The chart beneath it plots only the **share of creatives reporting each revenue stream** (2014 vs. 2022) — breadth of streams, not dollars. Having removed its income-quantification questions, the report is not in a position to say incomes "improved," and no data in either appendix supports the word. If the white paper quotes that insight, it should say what the underlying chart actually measures.

# CLAIM 2

> "No one has measured what an Austin musician earns since 2013."

## Verdict: Not defensible as written. The author is right that the phrasing is unfair.

The sentence is wrong in four distinguishable ways. **(a) The City of Austin published a musician wage for 2016 and 2017 on its own open data portal** — $17.49 and $17.68 median hourly for musicians and singers — bought from a commercial vendor. (b) At least two post-2013 efforts collected original earnings data from Austin musicians, both non-representative. (c) HAAM has published member income statistics annually throughout the period, from an income-screened program population. (d) Federal statistical agencies have measured earnings for this occupation in Texas continuously and without interruption since 2013 (Claim 3). What is true — and is the real finding — is narrower: **no representative, dollar-denominated measurement of Austin musician earnings has been produced by an Austin-specific survey since the 2014 census measured calendar-year 2013 income.**

If the paper wants a single sentence that survives scrutiny, use that one. If it wants the rhetorical force of the original, the honest version is: *"Austin's own music census has not asked a musician what they earn since 2013."* That is true, checkable, and squarely the point.

## Inventory of every post-2013 attempt found in the evidence base

Ordered by how close each comes to being an earnings measurement.

**First, a vintage point the paper has to settle explicitly.** The 2015 Austin Music Census was **fielded after 2013**: pretest November 4, 2014, survey open **November 12, 2014 – January 27, 2015** (`austin_music_census_2015_TitanMusicGroup_full.pdf` p.19), n = 3,968 usable responses (pp. 9, 20). But its income questions asked about **pre-tax calendar year 2013**. So whether the census satisfies a "measured since 2013" test depends on whether you mean fieldwork or reference period. Say which you mean; the sentence "no one has measured since 2013" quietly relies on the reference period while sounding like it describes fieldwork.

### A. Direct estimates of what an Austin musician earns, 2014 or later

**A1. UT Austin — "Support Austin Musicians" (Prof. Eric Drott et al.), drafted July 2024**
File: `study_2024_UT-Austin_SupportAustinMusicians.md`; source https://supportaustinmusicians.org/final-summary/
- *Measured:* self-reported annual income, per-set pay, gig frequency, insurance source, and policy preferences.
- *Method and sample:* qualitative interviews with **roughly 60 individuals** across genres. Non-random; no published sampling frame, response rate, or weighting. Project website, no named lead author, no peer review. **Interview field dates are not stated anywhere on the page** — the July 2024 date is when the summary was drafted, and the page cites data through 2024. Do not report a fieldwork period for it.
- *Found* (verified verbatim at the source on 2026-08-02): incomes with **"some earning as little as $15,000 and some $120,000 per year, but those at the high end of the spread represented outliers"**; **"Our most common income reported was $30,000-35,000, certainly below average for the Austin area"**; **"most citing $100"** as the typical rate paid per musician for a set; full-time musicians typically 2–4 shows a week; "16% have no insurance."
- *Status:* **the only post-2013 original collection of Austin musician annual-income data found in this evidence base.** Anecdotal in scale. Percentages from it must be written as "among roughly 60 musicians interviewed," never as Austin-wide.
- *Caution:* the page re-reports numbers from HAAM, the 2022 census, and news coverage without inline citation, and its demographic figures track the 2022 census closely — so much of it is compilation, not measurement. The most-common-income figure ($30,000–$35,000) is not comparable to the 2014 census's 68.4%-under-$10,000 because the census figure is **music-only** income and this appears to be **total** income.

**A2. KUT / KUTX *Pause/Play* informal poll, February 2022**
File: `news_2022-02-15_KUT_musician-pay-unchanged-40-years.md`
- *Measured:* per-gig pay.
- *Method:* the program "asked musicians how much they were making per gig." No n, no frame, no field dates published. Self-selected.
- *Found:* roughly **$100 per musician per gig**, consistent with the $50–$100 range the story documents from dated pay records.
- *Status:* journalistic canvass, not a survey. Usable as corroboration of a market norm, not as a measurement.

**A3. City of Austin — "Median Earnings of Creative Sector Occupations" (open data portal), published December 2018, covering 2016 and 2017**
File: `04_city_programs_lmf/socrata/2qxc-8cme_median_earnings_creative_occupations.csv`; portal https://data.austintexas.gov/d/2qxc-8cme

**This is the item the claim most clearly misses, and it appears in none of the folder's prior synthesis notes as an income measurement.** The City of Austin itself published median hourly earnings for musicians in Austin for two years after 2013.

- *Measured:* **median hourly earnings by SOC occupation** for 54 creative-sector occupations in Austin.
- *Found:* **Musicians and singers (SOC 27-2042): $17.49/hr in 2016 and $17.68/hr in 2017.** Music directors and composers (27-2041): $15.52 and $15.28. For context in the same file: fine artists $9.53, actors $15.76, photographers $15.68, graphic designers $20.66, sound engineering technicians $25.53 (2017 values).
- *Source and method, verified from the portal's own metadata on 2026-08-02:* the dataset is owned by **Austin Economic Development**, was created and last updated **2018-12-12**, and states that data "was gathered directly from the **Creative Vitality Suite** (cvsuite.org), a subscription-based… economic development tool," drawing on "**Economic Modeling Specialists International (EMSI)**, National Assembly of State Arts Agencies, National Center for Charitable Statistics." The stated purpose is explicitly about musician earnings: *"Median earnings of creative sector occupations tells us how much Austin's artists, musicians, and related creative sector workers are earning per hour."*
- *Status:* **a proprietary vendor-modeled estimate, not a survey and not a federal statistic.** EMSI's methodology is not public. But it is a real, City-published, post-2013 Austin musician earnings number, and the paper cannot say nobody produced one.
- *Why the level matters, and this is the substantive point:* the City's $17.68/hr for 2017 is **roughly half the OEWS figure for the same occupation, metro, and year** — OEWS Austin 2017 gives a median hourly wage of **$31.03** for musicians and singers (240 jobs). The gap is what you would expect if the vendor estimate incorporates self-employed and proprietor earnings, which OEWS excludes by construction. Two independent estimates of the same quantity differing by a factor of two is itself a finding about how poorly this workforce is measured, and it is worth putting in the paper.
- *Limits:* hourly, not annual — converting it requires an hours assumption the data does not supply. It covers only **2016 and 2017** and has not been refreshed since 2018-12-12. Some modeled values are implausibly low (craft artists $6.79/hr), which is a reason for caution about the whole file.
- ⚠️ **Geography is nowhere stated.** The description says "Austin's artists," but neither the metadata nor the column descriptions specify City of Austin versus Austin–Round Rock MSA. CVSuite regions are normally county- or MSA-based. **Do not assert a geography for this figure.**

**A3b. Companion dataset — the City's only *annual* dollar figure for Austin musicians**
File: `04_city_programs_lmf/socrata/mbwg-yjpy_creative_sector_industry_codes_2016.csv` — "Creative Sector Industry Codes, 2016," same department, same CVSuite/EMSI source, built for the City's 2016 Creative Economy Snapshot Report.

- **NAICS 711130 Musical groups and artists: 1,401 jobs (2016); earnings per job $20,495 (2015) → $22,697 (2016).**
- **NAICS 711510 Independent artists, writers, and performers: 14,823 jobs; $20,286 → $22,675.**
- 512240 Sound recording studios: 385 jobs; $25,927 → $25,495.
- *Limit:* these are **industry-level total earnings divided by jobs** from a vendor model — an average, not a median, not person-level, and "jobs" includes part-time and modeled self-employment. Still, this is the only annual dollar figure for Austin musical groups and artists anywhere in the City's open data, and it appears in none of the folder's prior synthesis notes.

### B. Administrative or program data on a self-selected program population

*Note on wording: these populations are screened in different ways, and the differences matter. HAAM screens on income (below 400% FPL). SIMS screens only on having earned some income in music. HOME serves musicians 55 and older. None is a sample of Austin musicians, and all will bias a reported income downward relative to the working population — but not by the same mechanism.*

*Verification provenance: every figure in this section was re-extracted from the primary filing or report for this fact-check. Infographic pages whose text layer scrambles the number-to-label pairing were rendered to PNG and read visually. The folder's `nonprofit_ecosystem_numbers.md` was not accepted on trust, and nine errors in it are listed at the end of this document.*

UT's figure matches HAAM's **2023** report (p.3) exactly, so UT is re-reporting HAAM's older number. Cite HAAM directly, with the year and threshold.

**B2. HOME (Housing Opportunities for Musicians and Entertainers), reported February 2023**
Source: Austin Chronicle, 2023-02-03, quoting HOME president Hanna Cofer.
- *Found:* **"50% [of HOME grant recipients] reporting they earn less than $1,500 per month including Social Security"**; 22% now live outside city limits.
- *Status:* grantee administrative data for musicians **aged 55 and older**. A real post-2013 Austin income statistic, but for a small, old, and means-tested subpopulation. Note the denominator includes Social Security income.

## FAIR FORMULATION — the true state of Austin musician income measurement since 2013

> Austin has not gone unmeasured since 2013, but it has gone un-*re*-measured in the one way that would matter. **(a) Representative earnings measurement:** there is none, and there never was — the 2014 Austin Music Census, the source of every widely cited Austin figure, was itself a self-selected online convenience sample (n=3,968) reporting pre-tax calendar-**2013** income. It described itself as establishing "a benchmark for future measurements," but its successor, the 2022 Greater Austin Music Census, removed the income-quantification questions on its community partners' advice, so the city's flagship instrument has produced no dollar figure for thirteen years. **(b) Anecdotal, modeled, and self-selected measurement:** several efforts have produced Austin musician earnings figures since 2013 and should be credited rather than ignored. **The City of Austin itself published one:** its open data portal carries median hourly earnings for musicians and singers in Austin of $17.49 (2016) and $17.68 (2017), bought from a commercial vendor, Creative Vitality Suite drawing on EMSI — roughly half the federal OEWS wage for the same occupation and year, because unlike OEWS it appears to count self-employed earnings. Beyond that: a University of Texas project interviewed roughly 60 Austin musicians and found reported incomes from $15,000 to $120,000 with $30,000–$35,000 most common; KUT's *Pause/Play* canvassed musicians in 2022 and found about $100 per musician per gig; HAAM publishes member income thresholds every year (half of members under $24,000 in 2025), though from an income-screened program population that cannot be generalized; and HOME reported in 2023 that half its grantees aged 55 and over earn under $1,500 a month. None is representative; sample sizes run from about 60 interviews to a few thousand self-selected respondents, and the City's figure is a proprietary model with no published methodology that stopped being updated in 2018. **(c) Ongoing federal statistical series:** meanwhile, the American Community Survey has measured earnings for musicians in Texas every year throughout this period, including self-employment income, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics has published an occupational wage estimate for Austin-metro musicians every year since 2005 — though only ever as an hourly rate, never an annual one, and only for the wage-and-salary slice. These have real limits for this workforce, but they are continuous, methodologically documented, and public. The accurate claim is therefore not that no one has measured what an Austin musician earns since 2013, but that **Austin's own music census stopped asking, and the city has since relied on a 2013 number, a handful of small non-representative studies, and federal series that were never designed to capture irregular self-employed performance income.**

# CLAIM 3

> Which federal series track musician earnings in Austin/Texas, at what frequency, and with what limitation.

## Verdict: Substantially confirmed, with three corrections to the claim as stated.

The claim's characterization of each series is right. Three things need fixing: **OEWS publishes no annual wage for musicians at all** (not "sometimes suppressed" — never, in any year, in any geography on disk); **BEA ACPSA does not reach Austin or any metro area**, only states and the nation; and **Nonemployer Statistics publishes no metro-level table** in what is held here, so an Austin figure has to be built by summing five counties.

### Geographic bottom line

Only **two** federal series produce a musician-specific number for the Austin metro: **OEWS** (hourly wage, W-2 workers only) and **ACS/PUMS** (via a PUMA crosswalk). QCEW reaches Travis County and the MSA but by industry, not occupation. Nonemployer Statistics reaches counties. BEA ACPSA stops at the state line.

### Verified occupation and industry codes

The paper should use these; two guesses in circulation are wrong.

| Concept | Code | Verified against |

| Musicians and Singers | SOC **27-2042** / ACS **OCCP 2752** / **SOCP 272042** | `02_pums_nes_microdata/PUMS_Data_Dictionary_2020-2024.pdf` (dictionary lines 5360–5361, 6747–6748) and the OEWS CSV |

### Series by series

**1. ACS / American Community Survey PUMS — annual, occupation-coded, includes self-employment income**
- *Measures:* person-level occupation, class of worker, total personal income (PINCP), personal earnings (PERNP = wages plus self-employment), hours, weeks. **The only source on disk that captures self-employment income for musicians.**
- *Geography:* Texas statewide; Austin MSA reachable through the 16-PUMA crosswalk at `02_pums_nes_microdata/puma_austin_msa_crosswalk_2020.csv` (Travis 9 PUMAs, Williamson 4, Hays 2, plus one combined Bastrop+Caldwell PUMA 05100 — those two counties cannot be separated).
- *Frequency and vintage:* annual releases; latest on disk is the **2020–2024 5-year** file.
- *Numbers* (`02_pums_nes_microdata/pums_musicians_creatives_TX_2020_2024_5yr.csv`): Texas musicians 885 unweighted / 19,256 weighted. **Austin 129 unweighted / 3,324 weighted** — 17.3% of the state's musicians against a 9.4% share of the state workforce, so musicians are about 1.8 times as concentrated in Austin as workers generally. On a base matched to the comparison population (employed, ages 18–64): **median total personal income $25,000 and median earnings $21,000, against $45,000 and $43,700 for all employed Texans 18–64.** Self-employment is the defining feature: **51.5% of Texas musicians and 72.2% of Austin musicians are self-employed, against 9.8% of the employed Texas baseline.**
- *Key limitation for a self-employed musician:* the ACS records only the **primary or most recent job**. A musician who gigs alongside a day job is coded to the day job and never appears as a musician. Counts are a lower bound and the sample is selected toward those for whom music is the main job. Austin's 129 unweighted records are also thin for cross-tabs.
- *Data-handling note found during verification:* the musician extract in the sibling folder is unrestricted (ages 16–92, including 206 not-in-labor-force and 32 unemployed records) while the comparison baseline is restricted to employed 18–64. The published $12,800 median-earnings figure is largely an artifact of including zero-earnings records; on a matched base it is $21,000. Dollars are nominal — ADJINC is present but not applied. **Flagged for the sibling folder's owner; the matched figures above are the ones to use.**

**2. BLS OEWS — annual, excludes the self-employed**
- *Measures:* employment and wage percentiles for wage-and-salary jobs, from an employer survey.
- *Geography:* Austin MSA (area 12420), Texas, and national, present in every year.
- *Frequency and vintage:* annual; **May 2025** latest; 21 vintages 2005–2025 on disk.
- *Numbers* (`01_wages_oews_qcew/oews_musicians_creatives_2005_2025.csv`): Austin 2025 median hourly **$31.49** across **120 jobs**, against an Austin all-occupation median of $27.38. Texas 1,430 jobs, median $33.59. National 36,180 jobs, mean $60.46.
- *Key limitation:* it excludes the self-employed entirely. **OEWS counts 120 musician jobs in Austin in 2025 against roughly 3,324 people in the PUMS estimate** — OEWS sees only the W-2 slice, on the order of one musician in twenty-five.
- **The annual-wage limitation is absolute, and this corrects the sibling folder's wording.** Annual mean and annual median are **blank in 63 of 63 musician rows** — every year, every geography. This is not partial suppression; BLS does not publish an annual wage for this occupation because the work is too irregular to annualize from an hourly rate. Actors (27-2011) are likewise blank in 39 of 39 rows. Any annual figure for Austin musicians derived from OEWS would have to be constructed by assumption, and the paper should not do it.
- *Two further gaps:* Austin has **no Music Directors and Composers rows for 2005–2007 or 2022–2025**, so that occupation has no current Austin estimate. Austin musician employment is also highly volatile (720 jobs in 2013, 100 in 2024, 120 in 2025), and the 2016 Austin row shows 430 jobs with all wage fields blank.

**3. BLS QCEW — quarterly, unemployment-insurance-covered employment only**
- *Measures:* establishments, employment, and wages for UI-covered payroll jobs, **by industry, not occupation.** It cannot isolate musicians as workers.
- *Geography:* Travis County (48453), Austin MSA (C1242 — note this does not join to OEWS's 12420), Texas, national. **Austin MSA rows stop at 2024**, one year behind OEWS.
- *Frequency and vintage:* quarterly, with annual averages used here; 2001–2025.
- *Numbers* (`01_wages_oews_qcew/qcew_arts_industries_2001_2025.csv`): Travis County NAICS 71113 (Musical Groups and Artists), private sector, grew from 39 to 56 establishments 2001–2025 while employment stayed flat (320 → 314) and average annual pay rose $27,142 → $56,461.
- *Key limitation:* it excludes sole proprietors and independent contractors — the dominant arrangement for musicians, and the majority of the population the paper is about.
- *Suppression trap:* at MSA level, NAICS 71113 is suppressed in **8 of 24 years** and 7115 in **10 of 24**. Suppressed rows carry `annual_avg_emplvl = 0` and `total_annual_wages = 0`, so anyone averaging without first filtering on `disclosure_code = "N"` will silently pull zeros into the series. **Also correcting the sibling folder:** it states the broader NAICS 71 aggregate is not suppressed at MSA level; it is, in 2012 and 2013.

**4. Census Nonemployer Statistics — annual, receipts rather than earnings**
- *Measures:* the count of businesses with no paid employees and their **total receipts**. Confirmed directly: `rcptot_thousands` is gross revenue in nominal thousands. **No cost, profit, or wage variable exists in the file.**
- *Geography:* **county and state only in this extract** — 965 county rows and 108 state rows, no MSA level. An Austin figure must be built by summing the five counties, which is clean here because none of the 7115 county-year cells is suppression-flagged.
- *Frequency and vintage:* annual, 2012–2023. **2023 is the latest; 2024 was checked and is not yet released.**
- *Numbers* (`02_pums_nes_microdata/nes_independent_artists_long.csv`): Travis County NAICS 7115 grew from 5,382 to 8,696 establishments 2012–2023 (+61.6%), receipts $116.1M → $298.7M. Texas grew 45,021 → 74,834 (+66.2%), receipts $787.7M → $1.86B, with a 2020 dip to 59,846. **Five-county Austin MSA aggregate: 7,186 establishments / $146.4M in 2012 to 12,911 / $425.6M in 2023, averaging about $32,962 in receipts per establishment.**
- *Key limitation:* this is the series that **does** see the self-employed, and that makes it tempting — but it counts **businesses, not people**, reports **revenue before any expense**, captures only activity reported to the IRS (cash gig work is missed), and bundles writers and all performers into 7115, so it is not musician-specific. The ~$32,962 average receipts figure is not income; set against the 2022 census finding that creatives spend an average of $10,500 a year on their music businesses, the gap between receipts and take-home is material and unmeasured.

**5. BEA ACPSA (Arts and Cultural Production Satellite Account) — annual, state and national only**
- *Measures:* value added, employment, and compensation for arts and cultural **industries**. No occupation detail and no musician category.
- *Geography:* **state and national only. It does not reach Austin or any metro area.** This corrects the claim as stated.
- *Frequency and vintage:* annual; state series 2001–2023, national 1998–2023. **2023 is the last release on disk.**
- *Codes:* BEA industry line codes, not NAICS or SOC — LineCode 111 Performing arts companies, 114 Independent artists/writers/performers.
- *Numbers* (`03_creative_economy_bea/tidy/`): Texas 2023 value added $65.6B, 360,964 jobs, $32.0B compensation, 2.5% of state GDP. Texas independent artists $1.64B / 6,610 jobs; performing arts companies $0.88B / 4,451 jobs.
- *Key limitation:* a macro accounting aggregate. It yields no individual earnings and cannot separate self-employed musicians.
- *Continuity risk, unverified:* the sibling folder's notes assert that BEA posted a February 2026 notice that it will no longer regularly produce ACPSA. **No copy of that notice is saved and it could not be verified from disk.** Confirm before describing ACPSA as ongoing.

**6. CPS — no usable Austin musician sample. Confirmed as far as it can be.**
- **No CPS data exists anywhere in this evidence base.** A search across 124 markdown, 74 CSV, and 123 PDF files found no CPS, ASEC, SIPP, IRS SOI, or County Business Patterns content.
- **The following is arithmetic inference, not a documented source, and should be presented that way.** The CPS monthly sample of roughly 60,000 households yields about 66,000 employed persons. Musicians are about 0.1% of employment (the PUMS Texas ratio is 0.142%), so roughly 66–94 musicians appear nationally per month. Austin is about 0.85% of US employment. That leaves **well under one Austin musician per monthly sample — on the order of 7 to 11 a year, or 34 to 56 pooled over five years.** That is below the 129 records the ACS already treats as thin for Austin cross-tabs.
- *Conclusion:* **CPS cannot support any Austin musician estimate.** It is usable for national musician figures at best. The paper should say so rather than cite it locally.

**7. Series not present on disk:** SIPP, IRS Statistics of Income, County Business Patterns. The 2017 Economic Census appears only secondhand, as the revenue basis inside the TXP consultant report. Also present but not musician-earnings series: BEA Regional Price Parities for MSA 12420 back to 2008 (`09_cost_of_living/bea_rpp_marpp_5metro_long.csv`) and ACS median rent and home value (`09_cost_of_living/acs_median_rent_homevalue_5geo_long.csv`) — these reach Austin but measure prices, not earnings.

## LAY EXPLANATION — what these series do and do not capture for a self-employed musician

> Picture a working Austin musician: a part-time day job, a dozen bar gigs a month, a few students, and some streaming royalties. Four federal statistics see different slices of that person, and none sees the whole. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' wage survey asks *employers* what they pay, so it sees only the W-2 sliver — it counted 120 musician jobs in the Austin metro in 2025, against roughly 3,300 people the Census Bureau estimates are working as musicians here, and it never publishes an annual salary for musicians in any city or any year, because the work is too irregular to annualize. The quarterly payroll series is built from unemployment-insurance records, so a self-employed performer is invisible to it by construction, and it tracks industries rather than the people in them. Census Nonemployer Statistics does see the self-employed, counting about 12,900 independent-artist businesses across the five-county Austin metro in 2023 — but it reports gross receipts before any expense, so it shows money coming in, not money kept, which matters when the same population reports spending about $10,500 a year on their music work. Only the American Community Survey asks people directly and adds self-employment income to wages, which makes it the closest thing to a real earnings measure — and even it records just one occupation per person, so our musician with a day job gets counted as whatever the day job is. The practical upshot: the federal system measures this workforce continuously, but it systematically under-counts it, and the more a musician looks like a self-employed patchworker, the more likely the statistics describe someone else.

# CLAIM 4

> "$15,475 = one saxophonist's 1981 salary from a bandleader's calendar."

## Verdict: Verified verbatim, at the live source.

**Outlet:** KUT 90.5 / KUTX (Austin's NPR station), from the *Pause/Play* podcast series
**Authors:** Elizabeth McQueen and Miles Bloxson
**Date:** February 15, 2022, 8:20 AM CST
**Title:** "Same as it ever was: Musician pay for live shows in Austin hasn't changed in 40 years"
**URL:** https://www.kut.org/austin/2022-02-15/same-as-it-ever-was-musician-pay-for-live-shows-in-austin-hasnt-changed-in-40-years
**Verified:** live retrieval 2026-08-02, byline and date confirmed as printed; local copy `news_2022-02-15_KUT_musician-pay-unchanged-40-years.md`

**Verbatim quote (21 words), musician Marcia Ball:**

> "And if you had worked for me for a full year as my sax player did, you would have made $15,475."

The preceding line establishes the document: *"I looked in another calendar from 1981,"* Ball says.

### How the number entered circulation as an Austin musician income figure

Through one sentence in the article itself. Immediately after quoting Ball, KUT writes:

> "But because the average pay for gigs has essentially stayed the same, many Austin musicians are still only making $15,475 a year."

That sentence performs the conversion. It takes a documented 1981 salary for one sideman in one bandleader's employ and, on the premise that nominal gig rates have not moved, restates it as a present-tense claim about many Austin musicians in 2022. The inference is a rhetorical bridge, not a measurement: no survey, no sample, no denominator.

### What claim it was used to support

**Nominal pay stagnation, with the inflation adjustment as the payoff.** KUT immediately adds: *"Adjusted for inflation, that would be about $47,865 today."* The point being made is that the same nominal work now buys about a third of what it did — roughly a 68% real decline. The $15,475 is the setup; $47,865 is the argument.

The article's actual documented finding is a range, not a point estimate: *"the pay for a gig in Austin has been the same for the last 40-plus years, averaging between $50 and $100 per gig, per musician."* This rests on four dated primary records — Marcia Ball's 1979 calendar ($300–$600 per night for a five-piece, which KUT divides five ways to get $60–$120 each, explicitly flagged as an assumption); Ball's 1981 calendar; drummer David Sanger's 1994 financial records ($75 for Jimmy LaFave at Shady Grove Unplugged, $80 for Mary Cutrufello at the Continental Club, $100 for Sarah Brown at Central Market); and Beto Martinez's recollection of $50 gigs in the early 2000s.

### Verdict on use

**Do not cite $15,475 as an Austin musician median, average, or current income.** It is one person's 1981 annual pay from one employer, sourced to a personal calendar. Propagation check, run for this fact-check: a string search across the full evidence base found `$15,475` in **no source document other than this one KUT article** — every other hit is a synthesis note in this folder discussing the KUT article, and the only two matches outside it are false positives inside longer numbers in unrelated CSVs (`100,47515` and `515,47549`). It appears **zero times** in the extracted text of the 2015 census and the 2022 Data Appendix, and the folder's 2014–2021 journalism index records that it appeared in none of the 19 articles retrieved from that period. So the figure has a single origin and, on this evidence, never propagated into any document that would give it standing. If the paper uses it, present it as what it is — a 1981 sideman's salary offered to illustrate nominal stagnation — and lead with the $47,865 real-terms comparison, which is the defensible part.

# CLAIM 5

> "Austin musician income figures circulating in 2026 journalism trace back to 2013 fieldwork."

## Verdict: Too strong, and it mis-diagnoses the problem. The defensible version is more interesting.

### What was tested

Every journalism or press item in this folder dated 2023-01-01 or later — the post-2022-census era — was read in full and every dollar figure extracted and traced. That is **13 items retrieved with content**, plus 3 that could not be retrieved (2 KVUE, 1 paywalled Billboard) and are excluded from the counts.

**Methodological caution applied:** the folder's article files contain a compiler's "Why it matters" section as well as verbatim article excerpts. Only figures appearing in the verbatim excerpt blocks were counted as figures the outlet actually published. This matters: "$15,000" appears in the compiler's framing of the KUT 2023-02-08 piece, but not in KUT's own reporting.

### The counts

**Items dated 2026 (n=2):** KUT, 2026-07-08, "Austin opens applications for $23 million in arts and music grants"; Austin Chronicle, 2026-07-16, "Your Live Music Fund FAQs, Answered."

> **Neither cites any Austin musician income figure — annual, monthly, or market per-gig. Zero of two.** Their dollar figures are entirely grant amounts ($23M total, $6M Live Music Fund, $5,000/$20,000 award tiers) and the City's administrative booking rate ($200 per musician for groups up to six, $150 for 7–10, $1,500 for 10+). So the claim as written cannot be tested on 2026 journalism, because 2026 journalism in this evidence base publishes no musician income figure to trace.

**Items dated 2023–2026 (n=13 retrieved).** Exactly **one** population-level Austin musician earnings figure appears anywhere in them:

| # | Figure | Item | Traces to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "almost one-third of Austin musicians earned **$15,000 or less** per year" | Austin Chronicle, 2023-02-03 | **2014 census → calendar-2013 income.** ✔ traces to 2013 |
| 2 | HOME grantees: "50% reporting they earn **less than $1,500 per month** including Social Security" | Austin Chronicle, 2023-02-03 | HOME program data, 2023, musicians 55+. ✘ not 2013 |
| 3 | First sync placement **$5,000**; Laboratorio paid artists **~$80,000** last year | KUT, 2025-06-23 | Individual and organizational anecdote, 2025. ✘ not 2013 |

So: **1 of 3 earnings figures in four years of coverage traces to 2013 fieldwork** — and the one that does was published by the Chronicle explicitly as an obsolete reference point, under the subhead **"No Data on Dollars,"** in the sentence *"The 2014 census had an easy and loud reference point that almost one-third of Austin musicians earned $15,000 or less per year. The lack of a follow-up inquiry on that matter means there's no opportunity to do an apples-to-apples comparison of earning power."* The Chronicle was flagging the staleness, not laundering it.

**Where 2013-derived figures actually did circulate: the 2022 wave, not 2026.** Two of the five 2022 items recycled census income statistics:
- KUT, 2022-02-15: *"20% of musicians are below the poverty line, and about another 30% hover just right above it"* — attributed on air to Nikki Rowling of Titan Music Group. Traces to the 2015 census, calendar-2013 income. ✔
- Austin Monitor, 2022-08-10: *"nearly one-third of local working musicians earned less than $15,000 annually"* — 2015 census. ✔

**What the "something else" is.** Across 2023–2026, the dollar figures Austin journalism now publishes about musicians are, in descending frequency: **City grant program amounts** (7 items), **festival and City pay rates** (5 items), **housing and home-price context** (2 items), **sector-wide economic impact** (1 item, PBS's unattributed "$1 billion annually"), and **individual artist anecdotes** (1 item). Earnings figures have not been replaced by newer earnings figures. They have been replaced by **program spending**.

### Defensible reformulation

> Every population-level Austin musician income figure still in circulation traces to the 2014 Austin Music Census, which measured pre-tax calendar-year 2013 income — making it thirteen years old. But the more telling pattern is that those figures have largely stopped circulating at all. In the twelve months to mid-2026, Austin journalism covering musicians published no musician income figure of any kind: coverage now reports grant dollars appropriated and the City's own booking rate rather than what musicians earn. When the Austin Chronicle last invoked the 2013 number, in February 2023, it did so under the subhead "No Data on Dollars" to make exactly this point — that the 2022 census left no successor and no apples-to-apples comparison is possible. The measurement gap has not produced stale numbers so much as an absence of numbers, and public debate has shifted to the one quantity that is still measured: how much the City spends.
