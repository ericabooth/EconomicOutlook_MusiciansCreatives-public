## PROVENANCE: the "$150/gig unchanged since the 1980s" claim

**This framing does not survive contact with the sources. It conflates three separate figures.** No retrieved source says musicians have been paid $150 a gig since the 1980s.

**1. The stagnant market-pay claim is $50–$100 per gig, not $150.**
KUT (2022-02-15) states the market rate "has been the same for the last 40-plus years, averaging between $50 and $100 per gig, per musician." KUT (2022-03-01) characterizes it as "~$100 per musician per gig." The supporting evidence is three primary records:
- Marcia Ball's **1979** calendar: $300/night typical band fee, up to $600; **$60–$120 per musician** after a five-way split (KUT's own arithmetic, explicitly labeled an assumption).
- Marcia Ball's **1981** calendar: her sax player earned **$15,475** for a full year.
- Drummer **David Sanger's 1994** financial records: **$75** (Jimmy LaFave, Shady Grove Unplugged), **$80** (Mary Cutrufello, Continental Club), **$100** (Sarah Brown, Central Market).
- Beto Martinez, early 2000s, recollection: **$50** per gig at age 19–20.

**2. The $150 is a City of Austin policy rate from 2016, not a market rate and not from the 1980s.**
KUT (2022-03-17): the Economic Development Department's Music and Entertainment Division paid **$150 per musician per hour since 2016**, and raised it to **$200** in March 2022. A separate **$150/hour** appears in the 2022 Live Music Fund draft framework as a *proposed* venue-payment rate (max $3,000/band). Both are administrative rates governing city-booked or city-funded performances only; the City does not regulate private venue pay.

**3. Unit confusion compounds the error.** The $150/$200 figures are **per musician per hour**. The $50–$100 figures are **per musician per gig**. They are not comparable without a set-length assumption.

**4. The current (2026) city rate schedule is not a flat $200 either.** Austin Chronicle (2026-07-16): **$200 per musician for groups up to six; $150 per musician for groups of 7–10; $1,500 flat for 10+**. Time unit not stated in that article — confirm against a City source.

**Recommended formulation:** "Austin's prevailing per-musician gig pay has been roughly $50–$100 for four decades, corroborated by dated pay records from 1979, 1981, 1994, and the early 2000s (KUT, 2022). Separately, the City's own booking rate for musicians it hires stood at $150 per musician per hour from 2016 until it rose to $200 in 2022."

## Cross-source discrepancies to resolve before publication

1. **Central-Austin musician decline: 10% or 12%?** KUT says 10%; Austin Chronicle and PBS say 12%. Two of three say 12%. Resolve against the 87-page census report.
2. **Census vintage labeling.** The first census is variously "2014" (fieldwork) and "2015" (release). Fix one convention.
3. **Prior-census sample size.** "Almost 4,000" (Chronicle) vs "~1,700 more than 2,200" (KUT, implying ~3,900). Minor, but pick one.
4. **KUT "45 gigs a month" for rent.** As extracted, $1,500 rent ÷ $100/gig = 15 gigs, not 45. Do not use until reconciled against the source audio.
5. **Live Music Fund annual totals use inconsistent frames.** $3.5M (2023) / $4.5M (2024) / "~$7M earmarked for live music" (2025) / $6M (2026). The 2025 figure may include programs the others exclude. Do not chart as a series without checking definitions.
6. **DEI findings characterized oppositely.** Juice Consulting press release: community "more positive than negative." Austin Chronicle: ageism, sexism, elitism are clear concerns and minority respondents rate treatment more harshly. Go to the report.
7. **UMAW's $385,495 vs $57,820 fee-to-payout estimate** is an advocacy organization's own unaudited calculation. Label it as such or drop it.

## Notable gap in the evidence base

**There is no post-2014 measurement of Austin musician annual earnings.** The 2022 census deliberately omitted the annual-earnings question (Austin Chronicle, 2023-02-03), so the widely cited "one-third earn $15,000 or less" figure is now more than a decade old and has no successor. Any 2026 claim about musician income levels rests on 2014 data, journalistic anecdote, or HOME's 55-and-older grantee subsample (50% under $1,500/month) — none of which is a current representative estimate. This is the single largest data gap on this beat and is worth stating explicitly in the white paper.
