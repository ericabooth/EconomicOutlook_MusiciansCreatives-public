# Austin Music Census: 2014 vs. 2022 — Extracted Figures

Compiled 2026-08-01 by direct extraction from the primary PDFs held in this folder.
Every number below was read off the source document (text layer or chart label), not from secondary coverage.

---

## 0. Provenance correction (important)

The white paper brief refers to the "2014 Austin Music Census (TXP Inc.)." That attribution is **wrong** and should be corrected before drafting:

- **The Austin Music Census (published June 1, 2015)** was authored by **Titan Music Group, LLC** for the City of Austin Economic Development Department's Music & Entertainment Division. Title page: "BY TITAN MUSIC GROUP, LLC." Copyright "©2015 Titan Music Group, LLC."
- **TXP, Inc.** authored the *separate* economic-impact series — an initial study in 2001, updates in 2007 and 2012, and *The Economic Impact of Music: 2016 UPDATE* (February 2016), which used 2014 data.
- Titan Music Group's principal, **Nikki Rowling**, later founded **Sound Music Cities**, which ran the 2022 census. The same analyst lineage across both censuses is why Sound Music Cities can publish direct 2014-vs-2022 comparison charts.
- Fielding vs. publication dates: the census survey was fielded **late 2014** (pretest Nov 4, 2014; focus groups Sept–Oct 2014) and **published June 2015**. Income questions asked about **pre-tax calendar year 2013**. So "the 2014 census" reports **2013 income**. Use that vintage carefully.

## 1. Samples and methodology

| | 2014 Census (Titan) | 2022 Census (Sound Music Cities) |
|---|---|---|
| Usable respondents | 3,968 | 2,260 |
| Completion rate | 66% | 71% (industry standard 42%) |
| Field period | Late 2014 (pretest Nov 4, 2014) | July 15 – Sept 12, 2022 |
| Published | June 1, 2015 | February 2023 |
| Geography | Austin area | Travis, Hays, Williamson, Bastrop, Caldwell counties |
| Extras | 1,501 free-form write-ins; 8 focus groups; 20+ interviews | 2 appendices (data deck, DEI deep dive); 50+ community partners |

**Respondent composition:**

| Category | 2014 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| Music Creative (performers/songwriters) | 60.0% (2,380) | 71% |
| Music Industry (owners/employees/contractors) | 35.3% (1,401) | 24% |
| Venue or Presenter | 4.75% (187) | 5% |

**Both are self-selected online convenience samples, not probability samples.** Neither supports population-level inference to "all Austin musicians." The 2022 report itself frames the comparison as showing "directional shifts," not precise change estimates.

### The single biggest methodological break

The 2022 census **deliberately removed the income-quantification questions** that the 2014 census used. From the 2022 Summary Report, p.2:

> "the 2022 survey updated demographic questions to include more diversity, equity, and inclusion elements and removed questions from the 2014 survey that aimed to quantify the income of music people in a way that turned off many respondents."

A respondent write-in in the 2022 Data Appendix (p.86) confirms the reception: *"I'm glad you did not ask about income. We've been in a pandemic for 2 1/2 years."* Another asked the opposite: *"I wish y'all would have asked how much of income goes towards rent."*

**Consequence for the white paper: there is NO 2022 equivalent to the 2014 median-income or poverty-rate statistics.** Any "musician incomes in Austin have/haven't changed since 2014" claim cannot be sourced to the two censuses side by side. The 2022 census measures affordability *stress* and *gig volume*, not dollars of income. This is a genuine evidence gap, and it is also why journalism from 2022–2026 keeps recycling the 2014 income numbers.

### Data-access caveat (2014 microdata)

The 2014 raw data was never released publicly. In October 2015, Texas State University professor Cindy Royal filed a Texas Public Information Act request for the de-identified quantitative data. Titan Music Group (a $45,000 city contract) argued the raw data was not subject to the Act and that release would "turn the entire process into an Internet parlor game of who is who." The City Attorney's office sided with disclosure. Source: GovTech, Oct 2015 (see `_sources.md`). Treat the published PDF as the authoritative and effectively only public version of the 2014 data.

### Means of employment (2014)

- 56% work another full- or part-time job in a **non**-music industry
- 15% work another full- or part-time job **within** the music industry
- **22.7% are full-time musicians**
- 3.5% currently unemployed
- 2.7% students
- Over 60% of all respondents (2,266 of 3,749) work **two or more jobs**
- 54% of all respondents have worked as musicians more than ten years

### Stagnating pay was already the top complaint in 2014

"Living and Affordability" ranked as having "Extreme or Strong Impact" more often than any other category. The highest-ranked single factor was **"Stagnating Pay,"** flagged by **87.3%** of musician respondents. This matters for the white paper's narrative arc: the stagnant-pay finding is not new in 2022 — it was the loudest signal in the 2014 data.

## 3. 2022 Census — what it does measure

### Housing and affordability

- **38%** are struggling to afford **rent/mortgage** — the top expense category. Others: dental/vision insurance 28%, health care 21%, health insurance 21%, nutritious food 17%, transportation 16%, prescription drugs 12%, child/elder care 7%.
- **44%** say their housing costs have gone up in the past two years; 33% no change; 17% moved once locally; **15% say housing costs caused their move**; 14% more than one local move; 8% moved here from elsewhere.
- Tenure: 60% renter, 47% homeowner, 3% stable but don't pay, 1% **no permanent or stable housing**. (Categories overlap / sum >100% as published.)
- Rent-vs-own vs. 2014: renting 56.8% → 53.2%; owning 43.2% → 46.8%. Ownership slightly up — cuts against a pure displacement story and is worth flagging.
- Distance from downtown (2022): average **10.7 miles**, median **8 miles**. Distribution: 0–1 mi 7%, 2–3 mi 15%, 4–5 mi 18%, 6–10 mi 26%, 11–20 mi 22%, 21–30 mi 9%, 31+ mi 3%.
- Distance by subgroup (average miles): overall 10.6; venue owners 6.6; heterosexual 8.7; <3 yrs experience 8.9; ages 18–24 11.8; ages 55–64 11.8; 6–10 yrs experience 12.3; **no health insurance 12.6**; **BIPOC 12.9**.
- 73% reside within the City of Austin; 27% outside.
- Context cited in the report (American Growth Project via KXAN, Oct 2022): Austin median home value rose from **$349,156 (Aug 2020) to $566,479 (Aug 2022)**, with average multifamily rents rising 10% year over year.

### Leaving Austin

From Summary Report Insight 1 (p.7):

> "89% of music census respondents are planning to continue their music work in the next 3 years, but only 64% say they will continue to live in the greater Austin region during this time, indicating that a third of the ecosystem is considering not just moving further from central Austin, but out of the metro area completely."

So: **89% intend to keep working in music; only 64% intend to still be in greater Austin in three years** — an implied ~25-point gap, characterized by the authors as roughly a third of the ecosystem considering leaving the metro. (Austin Chronicle coverage renders this as "36% plan to leave the Austin area in the next three years." Both framings trace to the same item; prefer the 89%/64% pair, which is what the report actually prints.)

### Gig volume — the closest thing to a pay proxy

Local paid gigs per month, music creatives only:

| Gigs/month | 2014 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| 0 shows | 7.6% | **14%** |
| 1–3 shows | 49.3% | 51% |
| 4–6 shows | 21.9% | 18% |
| 7–10 shows | 11.3% | 8% |
| 11–15 shows | 5.4% | 5% |
| 16+ shows | 4.7% | 4% |
| **More than 3 shows** | **43%** | **35%** |

Sound Music Cities' own framing: 0 gigs **+6 points**, more than 3 gigs **−8 points**, "Local gigs are declining since 2014."

Pandemic-adjusted: restricting 2022 to creatives who said their work had returned to pre-pandemic levels, "more than 3 gigs" is 38% (−5 points vs. 2014) rather than 35%. Among those *not* recovered it is 29%. Split on the recovery question: **67% yes, 29% no**.

- **54% of gigs pay a base guarantee** (the rest are percentage/door deals).
- The report notes musicians' key earnings come from **1–3 paid gigs per month** — the modal category.

### Costs borne by creatives (average annual, 2022)

New music production **$3,538**; workspace $1,390; gear rentals $1,378; promotion $1,350; merch $921; supplies $865; accounting/legal $585; social media/web $473.
**Average total annual spend $10,500**, of which **60% is spent locally ($6,300)**.

Set this against the 2014 finding that 68.4% earned under $10,000 from music: for a large share of the population, annual business expenses are of the same order as annual music revenue.

### Work structure and safety net

- 52% operate primarily as **freelancers**
- 38% supplement income with work **outside** the creative industry
- 77% of music training is experience-based rather than formal
- 84% want more **grants**
- **84% have health insurance, up 5 points since 2014**, which the report credits to access through HAAM
- COVID relief: **61% received none**; 26% received it through a music job; 11% other; 8% through a non-music job. Of those who got it through music, **80% said it was the first government support they had ever received as a music professional** (7% no, 13% not sure). The report reads this as evidence of "how rarely music people have been able to access the social safety net."

### Sources of income — share reporting each stream (creatives)

| Stream | 2014 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|
| Live gigs (local) | 89.1% | 92.0% |
| Live gigs (road) | 78.7% | 75.0% |
| Merch | 63.2% | 71.0% |
| Recordings | 42.0% | 58.0% |
| Teaching | 35.1% | 56.0% |
| Songwriting | 27.1% | 51.0% |
| Studio | 34.2% | 41.0% |

Diversification is the clearest positive signal in the 2022 data — more creatives report more streams. Note this measures **breadth of streams, not dollars**, so it cannot show whether total income rose.

### Demographics, 2014 → 2022

- White/European 67.0% → 60.3%; Hispanic 10.4% → 15.4%; Black/African American 3.6% → 5.9%; Asian 1.6% → 3.8%; two or more races → 4.2%; MENA → 0.6%; prefer not to say 8.6% → 9.0%
- Male 71% → 62%; female 29% → 29%; non-binary 4%; prefer not to say 4%
- Ages 18–24: 7% → 6%; 25–39: 49% → **38%**; 40–54: 28% → 33%; 55–64: 13% → 13%; **65+: 3% → 9%**
- Years working in music — under 3 years: 14% → **6.6%**; 3–5 years: 17.7% → 9.9%; 6–10: 19.2% → 18.0%; **10+ years: 49.2% → 65.5%**

The aging/entry pattern is one of the strongest findings: the share with under three years in the industry more than halved while the 10+ year share rose ~16 points. Sound Music Cities' reading: "Fewer younger people are entering the industry, shrinking the industry overall." Caveat: differential survey response by tenure could produce part of this, since the 2022 outreach ran through established community partners.

### Venues and presenters (2022)

- Venue types: live music venue 82%, bar 42%, **community space 35% (up from 6% in 2014)**, other 21%, restaurant 18%, coffee shop 12%
- **Only 17% of venues are non-profits**, while most support funding requires non-profit status — a structural barrier the report flags explicitly
- Local talent as share of bookings in the most recent quarter: 75–100% for 55% of venues; 50–74% for 22%; 25–49% for 8%; 1–24% for 14%; 0% for 1%
- Intent to book more local artists over the next three years: definitely yes 44%, probably yes 32%, don't know 19%, probably no 5%, definitely no ~0%
- Music-industry sub-sectors represented: presenters/venues 43%, recording 34%, music marketing 31%, production support 31%, music development 27%, music media 22%, agents and business 21%, label distribution 15%, wholesale retail 13%, music publishing 11%, manufacturing 7%, public sector 6%, medical 3%

### DEI module (new in 2022, 14 adjective pairs)

Most negatively scored: elitist 59%, sexist 54%, ageist 52%, competitive 51%, homogenous 46%. Most positively scored: non-homophobic 56%, inclusive 54%, welcoming 52%, friendly 51%, safe 49%. Subgroup detail: women scored the scene as homophobic about as negatively as the queer community did, and the queer community scored it as sexist about as negatively as women did.

#### Economic precarity is the strongest single predictor of a negative experience

The DEI appendix crosstabs each adjective by ~24 subgroups, including two economic-hardship proxies — **"No Health Insurance"** and **"Rent Home."** The uninsured group is the **most negative subgroup on essentially every dimension measured**, ahead of race, gender, and sexual-orientation groupings:

| Dimension (negative pole) | Overall | No health insurance | BIPOC | Renters | Home owners |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elitist | 59% | **76%** | 70% | 67% | 51% |
| Ageist | 52% | **62%** | 57% | 55% | 49% |
| Individualistic | 40% | **57%** | 55% | 45% | 36% |
| Unsupportive | 28% | **46%** | 40% | 32% | 23% |
| Unwelcoming | 27% | **45%** | 41% | 35% | 21% |
| Uncomfortable (vs. safe) | 25% | **43%** | 38% | 31% | 20% |
| Homophobic | 16% | **26%** | 24% | 21% | 11% |

The renter-vs-owner gap runs in the same direction on every row, typically 10–16 points.

**Why this matters for the white paper:** the 2022 census dropped income questions, but insurance status and housing tenure survive as usable proxies for economic standing — and they sort experience of the scene more sharply than the demographic categories the DEI module was designed around. The scene is not experienced as "elitist" uniformly; it is experienced that way overwhelmingly by the people who cannot afford health coverage (76% vs. 51% for homeowners). This is the closest the 2022 data comes to an income gradient, and it is worth reconstructing rather than leaving in the DEI appendix where few readers will find it.

Caveat: these are cross-tabulations of attitude items, not causal estimates, and the subgroups overlap heavily (uninsured respondents are disproportionately renters and disproportionately BIPOC). No multivariate analysis was published. Treat as descriptive.

Source: `austin_music_census_2022_DEI_APPENDIX.pdf`, pp. 9–15 (image-only PDF; values read from chart labels).

## 4. TXP economic impact — the number behind "$1.8 billion"

From *The Economic Impact of Music: 2016 UPDATE* (TXP, Inc., February 2016), using 2014 data:

| | 2010 | 2014 |
|---|---|---|
| Output | $1.6 billion | **$1,831.7 million** |
| Value-added | — | $1,071.3 million |
| Earnings | — | $530.6 million |
| Jobs | 18,000+ | **20,723** |
| City of Austin tax revenue | ~$40 million | $47.13 million |

**The critical detail that the "$1.8 billion industry" headline hides:** TXP found that over two-thirds of the jobs are now on the *tourism* side, and that between 2010 and 2014 —

- music-related **tourism** added **3,780 jobs (+37%)**, while
- **music and its secondary effects lost 1,205 jobs.**

So the sector's headline growth was driven by music *tourism*, not by music production or performance. The "booming industry, broke musicians" framing that dominates 2015–2016 journalism is directly supported by TXP's own decomposition, and this is probably the strongest single piece of evidence in the folder for the white paper's core tension.

TXP defines "music" as (1) primary music — production, music video, industry, tour and recording services, performers, commercial music; and (2) music-related tourism. Tourism attribution relies on Austin Convention & Visitors Bureau attraction-appeal surveys normalized against Dean Runyan tourism data. That attribution step is an assumption, not a measurement, and roughly two-thirds of the headline impact rests on it.

## 6. Where the "$150 a gig / unchanged since the 1980s" claim actually comes from

The claim in circulation conflates two distinct things, and the white paper should separate them:

1. **Market gig pay.** KUT (Feb 15, 2022) reported that Austin live-show pay has been roughly flat for 40+ years at about **$50–$100 per musician per gig**, evidenced by drummer David Sanger's 1994 pay records ($75, $80, $100 for specific gigs). This is documentary and anecdotal, not survey-based. The 2014 census corroborates the *perception* — 87.3% named stagnating pay as an extreme or strong impact — but neither census published a per-gig dollar figure.
2. **City-event pay.** The **$150** figure is a *City of Austin booking rate*, not a market average — a base rate in place since 2016, later raised. Council subsequently set a **$200** standard minimum for musicians at city events. Support Austin Musicians describes the current standard as **$200/hour per musician for city-organized events (updated 2019)**, noting it "has not been widely adopted by private venues."

**Recommendation: do not write "$150 per gig hasn't changed since the 1980s" as a factual claim.** It merges a municipal booking rate with a market norm and attaches a decade the sources don't support. The defensible version is: market pay per musician per gig has been roughly $50–$100 for four decades per KUT's reporting, while the City's own booking floor moved from $150 (2016) to $200. See the journalism files in this folder for full provenance.

### The $15,475 figure — traced, and it is not what people think

The **$15,475** annual-income figure circulating in 2022 coverage **does not appear in either census**. Its actual origin, confirmed from the KUT article text (see `news_2022-02-15_KUT_musician-pay-unchanged-40-years.md`):

> "I looked in another calendar from 1981," Marcia Ball says. "And if you had worked for me for a full year as my sax player did, you would have made $15,475."

KUT then writes: *"because the average pay for gigs has essentially stayed the same, many Austin musicians are still only making $15,475 a year."*

So $15,475 is **one saxophone player's 1981 annual earnings from one bandleader's personal calendar**, which KUT used rhetorically to illustrate stagnation. It is **not a median, not a survey statistic, and not a 2022 measurement.** KUT itself notes that adjusted for inflation the 1981 sum would be about $47,865 in 2022 dollars — which is the actual point being made.

**Do not cite $15,475 as an Austin musician median income.** If the white paper uses it at all, present it as what it is: a 1981 sideman's annual pay, offered as an illustration of nominal stagnation. The census's own nearest comparator is the Texas full-time minimum wage of $15,080 (2014).
