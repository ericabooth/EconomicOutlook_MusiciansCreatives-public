# National Musician-Pay Surveys and Other-City Music Censuses

Evidence file for the Texas 2036 white paper on the economics of working musicians in Austin and Texas.

**Compiled:** 2026-08-01. All retrieval dates below are 2026-08-01 unless noted.
**Scope:** national musician-pay surveys and music censuses for cities *other than* Austin, assembled for benchmarking. Austin's own 2014 and 2022 censuses are documented in the sibling file `austin_music_census_2014_vs_2022_extracted_numbers.md`; Austin figures appear here only as anchor rows in the §6c comparison table, sourced to that file.
**Rule applied throughout:** no number, URL, or finding in this file is inferred or reconstructed. Where a figure was not published, the cell says so. Blanks are real blanks.

---

## Reader's warnings that apply to everything below

These five caveats govern how any of these numbers may be used in the paper.

1. **Almost every source here is a non-probability, self-selected convenience sample.** None of the music censuses, and neither MIRA nor the Future of Music Coalition survey, drew a random sample of musicians. They cannot be read as population estimates for a city's or the nation's musicians. The American Community Survey (ACS) occupation data is the only representative benchmark that appears in these documents, and it appears only inside the MIRA report.
2. **Means and medians are mixed across sources, and the gap between them is large.** Sound Music Cities reports *averages* for every city; the Future of Music Coalition website reports averages; MIRA reports medians. Musician income is heavily right-skewed, so these are not interchangeable. Two places in this file show the size of the gap directly. The DC census published both for gig pay: average local gig $446 against a median of $150. And the peer-reviewed version of the FMC survey (DiCola 2013, §2b) reports a median estimated music income of **$18,000** against the mean of **$34,456** that the FMC website published. **Wherever a median exists, use it.**
3. **Vintages span 2011 to 2025 and nominal dollars are not deflated.** The Future of Music Coalition survey fielded in 2011, MIRA in 2018, the city censuses between October 2022 and 2025. Any cross-source dollar comparison needs a deflator applied first.
4. **"Considering leaving" is not one measured quantity.** Some censuses publish an explicit "uncertain or considering leaving" percentage; others publish only "% confident about staying"; others publish neither. These are different questions. The comparison table below keeps them in separate columns for that reason.
5. **The streaming and advocacy figures in §5 are not measurements.** UMAW, the Music Workers Alliance, and the WIPO study all trace their per-stream rates back to a single non-audited blog aggregation (The Trichordist's Streaming Price Bible), and no advocacy organization in this file has ever fielded an earnings survey of its own members. Use §5 to describe the *policy debate*; do not use it to state what musicians earn. Earnings claims should rest on OEWS, QCEW, and PUMS, held in the sibling evidence folders.

### Sample and method

- **N = 1,227** musicians and composers, completed online interviews.
- **Fieldwork:** April 12 to June 2, 2018.
- **Target population:** "Individuals who earned a living as a musician or composer, or who were endeavoring to earn a living through making music."
- **Nonrandom sample**, recruited from MusiCares clients (62%), American List Council (30%), and referrals (8%). The MusiCares-heavy frame matters: MusiCares is a musician assistance charity, so the frame is weighted toward musicians who have sought support.
- Many questions were lifted from national surveys so results could be compared to the general public.
- Income tables are based on a smaller analytic sample: Table 1 notes "Samples include up to 787 observations."

### Income findings, verbatim

- ACS benchmark: "The Appendix Table indicates that the median musician in the U.S. earned between $20,000 and $25,000 a year in the period from 2012 to 2016, according to ACS data." (SOC 27-2040, musicians, singers and related workers, including composers and music directors.)
- "The median musician in our sample reported earning nearly $35,000 in 2017." **This is total personal income from all sources, not music income.**
- "The MIRA Musician Survey found that the average musician earns two-thirds of their annual income from music-related activities, and one third from non-music related sources."
- "Five percent of the sample earned less than $100 from music-related activities in 2017."
- "For those who earned more than $100 in music activities, the average share of income from music-related activities was 82 percent."
- **"The median musician in the MIRA Musician Survey earned $21,300 from music-related sources in 2017."** Footnote: "This figure increases to $22,500 if the sample is restricted to those who earned more than $100."
- "The median musician earned income from 3.5 different sources in 2017, and the average musician earned income from 3.0 different sources."
- "Sixty-one percent of musicians said that their music-related income is not sufficient to meet their living expenses."
- "When asked about their total income, including non-music related income, 36 percent of musicians said their total income was insufficient to meet their living expenses. Half of Non-whites, and 32 percent of Whites, said their total income was insufficient."

> **Disambiguation note, important for citation accuracy.** Press coverage of this report conflates three different numbers. The widely quoted "$20,000 to $25,000" is the *ACS* figure for all U.S. musicians' *total personal income*, not a MIRA survey result. The MIRA sample's own median *total* income was ~$35,000. The MIRA sample's median *music-related* income was $21,300. Digital Music News, for example, describes $21,300 as an "average" when the report calls it a median. Cite the report, not the coverage.

### Health insurance, verbatim

- "The FMC survey found that 57 percent of musicians had health insurance coverage in 2013."
- Split-sample design: half the sample got the Future of Music Coalition question wording, half got the Census Bureau wording. "The FMC version of the question indicated that 87.7 percent of musicians had health insurance in 2018, and the Census Bureau's variant of the question indicated that 84.6 percent of musicians had health insurance in 2018."
- "About half of musicians with health insurance coverage indicated that they purchased private insurance or were covered by Medicaid, which are two sources of coverage that were expanded under the Affordable Care Act."
- "The health insurance coverage rate for the U.S. workers age 19 to 64 as a whole was 88.8 percent in 2016 according to the latest Census Bureau data."
- "The health insurance that musicians are covered by may be inadequate for their needs in many cases, but if the MIRA and FMC survey results are even roughly accurate, they suggest that there has been a major increase in health insurance coverage for musicians since the Affordable Care Act went into effect."

### Mental health, discrimination, time use

- "Half of musicians reported feeling down, depressed or hopeless at least several days in the last two weeks, compared with less than a quarter of the adult population as a whole."
- 11.8% "thought about dying or hurting themselves in at least several days in the last two weeks."
- Prescription medication used on a regular basis: depression 13%, anxiety 12%, sleep 10%, pain 8%, beta-blockers 8%, ADD 3%.
- 72% of female musicians reported sex discrimination (against 27.8% of female U.S. self-employed workers); 67% reported sexual harassment.
- 63.3% of non-white musicians reported racial discrimination, against 36% of non-white self-employed workers.
- Time use in the preceding week: 14 hours performing, 5.7 hours performance-related travel, 4.4 hours composing. "Musicians, on average, spend more than half as much time earning income from non-music related work, as they do from performances."
- Top genres performed last year: Classical 37%, Jazz 35%, Pop 35%, Folk 31%, Blues 31%, Country 28%, Christian 27%, Adult Contemporary 24%, Independent 23%, Mainstream Rock 23%.
- Primary instruments: guitar 25%, piano/keyboard 17%, voice 15%, drums 10%, bass 10%, organ 5%.

### Sample and method, verbatim

- "The Money from Music survey was open from September 6 – October 28, 2011."
- "The survey was open to all musicians, composers and performers aged 18 years or older who were US citizens or residents."
- **"5,371 musicians and composers completed the survey."** "Over 6,700 eligible musicians took at least part of the survey. A total of 5,371 musicians completed the central questions about revenue, while 4,652 musicians made it through every single question in the survey. Thus, depending on the question, we report responses based on a total population of somewhere between 4,652 and 5,371 musicians."
- Three data collection methods: interviews with about 80 US-based musicians and composers, financial case studies from verifiable bookkeeping data, and the online survey.
- The project identified **42 distinct revenue streams** available to US musicians.

### Income findings, verbatim

- "Average age of survey takers: 45.2." Range 18 to 100; the most-represented band was 55 to 59.
- "40 percent said they spend more than 36 hours a week or more doing music."
- **"42 percent said they derived *all* of their personal income from music."**
- **"Average personal gross income of survey respondents was $55,561."** Measured as a banded estimate from "less than $20,000" to "more than $200,000" in $20,000 increments. "This is slightly higher than the US population."
- **"The average estimated music income was $34,455."** Method, verbatim: "By multiplying personal gross income by % of income derived from music, we were able to calculate an average estimated music income for all respondents of $34,455. This is slightly lower than the US average but this estimated music income calculation shifts significantly based on a musician's role, experience, education and genre."

> **Correction flag.** Some secondary summaries describe $55,561 as a *median*. The source explicitly calls it an **average**. Both FMC headline income figures are means, not medians.

- Distribution, verbatim: "Two-thirds – or 66% – of survey respondents reported that 0% of their income was derived from sound recordings in the past 12 months, and another 22% said it was less than 10% of their income. - Trend, verbatim: "Of 4,447 respondents, 16% said their income from sound recordings had increased, 18% said it had stayed the same, and 22% reported that it had decreased over the past five years… This suggests that, in aggregate, survey respondents' income from sound recordings has been declining." - Merchandise: "Only 12 percent of artists surveyed reported making any money from merchandise in the previous 12 months, with merchandise accounting for just 2 percent of all respondents' income." By genre, merchandise share of total revenue: rock 6%, hip hop 5%, country 2%, jazz and classical each under 1%.

> **Gross, not net.** FMC's own footnote is essential and should be carried into the paper: "all of the estimated music related income reported by survey respondents is gross income. That is, all of their music income before expenses. For some kinds of musicians, such as bandleaders or touring bands, expenses can be significant and include items such as fees paid to band members, travel expenses, and recording expenses. While the Money from Music Survey does not ask questions about expenses, the Individual Artist Case Studies do examine this critical area in great detail." The same caveat applies to every city music census in §4.

### Sample composition, verbatim, and why it matters

- Primary genre: Classical 34.7%, Jazz 16.2%, Rock/Alt-rock 7.2%, Pop 4.5%, Composer 4.3%, Singer-songwriter 3.5%, Folk 2.3%, Indie 2.2%, Americana 2.1%, Country 1.8%.
- "80 percent have a college degree or higher." "58% of respondents attended a music school or conservatory and 61% of respondents earned a degree in music."
- "Over half of survey respondents reported having 25 years or more of experience."

> **This sample is more than half classical and jazz, four-fifths college-educated, and averages 45 years old.** That composition is the single biggest reason FMC's income figures run high, and it is why the aggregate 28% live-performance share is far below MIRA's 41.6%. Do not treat FMC's population as comparable to a city music census population, which skews toward gigging popular-genre musicians.

### 2b. DiCola (2013) — the peer-reviewed version, which publishes the medians the project website never did

**Full citation:** DiCola, Peter. "Money from Music: Survey Evidence on Musicians' Revenue and Lessons About Copyright Incentives." *Arizona Law Review* 55, no. 2 (2013): 301–370. Associate Professor, Northwestern University School of Law. https://arizonalawreview.org/pdf/55-2/55arizlrev301.pdf | **Local file:** `fmc_dicola_money_from_music_ArizLRev_2013.pdf` (579,507 bytes, 70 pp.)

DiCola co-developed and analyzed the same 2011 Money from Music survey with FMC's Cook and Thomson. **This is the highest-quality citable version of the FMC data and it should be preferred over the project website**, because it reports medians, states its imputation method, and was peer-reviewed.

**Abstract, verbatim:** "Based on an original, nationwide survey of more than 5,000 musicians, this Article addresses one of the key links in the incentive theory's chain of logic. For most musicians, copyright does not provide much of a direct financial reward for what they are producing currently. The survey findings are instead consistent with a winner-take-all or superstar model in which copyright motivates musicians through the promise of large rewards in the future in the rare event of wide popularity."

**Income, verbatim — note the median, which is the number to use:**

- Total personal income from all sources: "The median annual income was $50,000, and the mean was $55,561. Thus, our sample includes people with relatively high incomes, compared to the general population."
- Estimated music income, N = 5,013: **"The median of the distribution is $18,000; the mean is $34,456."**
- Method, verbatim: "I did this calculation by picking points within each of the personal annual income ranges and multiplying by the share of income derived from music." For the top open band, "we used a midpoint of $330,000."

> **The median music income from the FMC survey is $18,000, not $34,455.** The project website published only the mean. The mean is nearly double the median, which is exactly what a superstar distribution produces. Any benchmark drawn from this survey should use $18,000, or report both with the skew noted.

**Full eight-category revenue allocation, verbatim** — this is the cleanest single statement of musician income composition in the whole evidence file:

| Revenue category | Share of average musician's music revenue |
|---|---|
| Live performance | 28% |
| Teaching | 22% |
| Salaries (orchestras, chamber ensembles, bands) | 19% |
| Session work | 10% |
| Other | 7% |
| Compositions | 6% |
| Sound recordings | 6% |
| Merchandise | 2% |

Verbatim: "the largest revenue category for musicians is live performance (28%). Other relatively important revenue streams, on average, are teaching (22%); salaries, primarily for those in orchestras, chamber ensembles, or bands (19%); and session work (10%). Revenue from compositions and sound recordings each accounts for only 6% of the average musicians' revenue from music, a total of approximately 12%. Merchandise generated only 2% of revenue, on average."

**Copyright-relatedness of musician revenue, verbatim:** "Among all respondents, in aggregate, the shares of revenue from compositions and recordings add up to **12% of revenue that is directly related to copyright. Ten percent of revenue has a mixed relationship to copyright. And 78% of revenue has an indirect or no relationship to copyright.** These figures provide important context for policy decisions. From the perspective of most musicians, or the average musician (which might be a misleading concept), copyright law is only directly responsible for one-tenth to one-fifth of their revenue."

**Work intensity and full-time status, verbatim:**

- "42.1% of all respondents earn 100% of their income solely from music."
- "One possible definition of 'full-time' musicians would include those who spend 36 or more hours per week on music and who derive 75% or more of their income from music. **I find that 32.3% of respondents meet that particular characterization of a 'full-time musician.'**"
- Hours: "Just over a quarter of respondents spend 15 hours per week or less on music; a similar proportion spend 16–30 hours per week; a little less than a quarter of respondents spend 31–45 hours per week; and a little more than one-fifth of respondents spend 46 or more hours per week."

**Hourly wage, with an external validity check, verbatim:** "The government estimate of the mean wage for musicians is $31.74 per hour, with a median of $22.99" (BLS Occupational Employment Statistics, "Musicians and Singers," employees only, self-employed excluded). "Among the subset of respondents in the sample who collect some part of their income as salaried musicians (usually as orchestra players), the estimated mean wage is $28.91 per hour, with a median of $20.07. The proximity of the survey estimate to that of the Bureau of Labor Statistics provides some confidence in the representativeness of the sample."

**Sample composition, verbatim:** "about 88% of respondents were white." "Across the entire sample, 48% of respondents" reported classical as a primary genre. "almost 74% of classical musicians, jazz musicians, and composers attended a music school or conservatory, and almost 80% of those respondents earned a" music degree. Response funnel: "there remained 6,769 eligible respondents, or 91.5%" after screening; the article documents a selection effect by survey-length version (5.5% breakoff on the short version against 2.9% medium and 1.0% long).

### 5a. Citigroup, 2018 — "Putting the Band Back Together: Remastering the World of Music"

**Full citation:** Bazinet, Jason (lead author). *Putting the Band Back Together: Remastering the World of Music.* Citi GPS: Global Perspectives & Solutions, August 6, 2018. 88 pp. **Publicly available.** https://www.citigroup.com/global/insights/music-industry | **Local file:** `citi_putting_the_band_back_together_2018.pdf` (3.1 MB, 88 pp.)

> 🔴 **The direction of this statistic is almost always reported backwards.** Citi's own framing is that the artist share **ROSE**, from 7% in 2000 to 12% in 2017. It is routinely cited as evidence of decline. If the paper cites it, it must not imply a downward trend.

**Headline claim, verbatim:** "Artists' share of music revenues is small. In 2017, artists captured just 12% of music revenue with most of the value leakage driven by the costs of running a myriad of distribution platforms — AM/FM radio, satellite radio, Internet distributors — augmented by the costs (and profits) of the record labels."

**Trend, verbatim:** "The proportion captured by artists is, however, on the rise (it was just 7% of industry revenues in 2000). The bulk of the improvement is not driven by the growth in music subscription services. Rather, it's driven by the strength in the concert business. Music labels act as intermediaries for subscription services (Apple, Spotify) but are largely excluded from the economics of the concert business. As such, growth in concert revenue is particularly helpful to artists." Elsewhere: "Over the last 20 years, the artist's share of the industry's revenues has hovered between 7% and 12%."

**The arithmetic, verbatim** (Figures 72 and 73):

- "In 2017, out of $43 billion of potential revenues, only $18.2 billion was available for the labels, artists and the artists' support staff." Citi arrives there by removing retailer mark-up, concert and venue costs, distribution platform costs, and distribution platform EBITDA.
- "Next, we remove the fees from the royalty collection entities (HFA, PROs, SoundExchange), costs for the artist's manager, record producer, concert promoter, concert agents and record labels. The balance is available to the artist. **In 2017, we estimate about $5.0 billion flowed to the artist, ~12% of total revenues.**"

**The $43.0 billion denominator, as printed in Figure 72 ($ millions):**

| Component | Revenue |
|---|---|
| Physical purchase | 1,569 |
| Digital purchase | 1,531 |
| Satellite subscription | 5,432 |
| Digital subscription | 4,092 |
| Concert | 8,000 |
| **Audio ads (radio station and web delivery)** | **14,984** |
| Audio ads (pure web players) | 1,147 |
| Video ads (YouTube) | 806 |
| Music publishing (B2B) | 2,312 |
| Licensing, artist services, expanded rights (B2B) | 3,148 |
| **Total music revenues** | **43,020** |

> ⚠️ **Methodological objection, now verifiable against the primary source.** Billboard's critique was that "the bank arrives at its 12 percent number by counting revenue from radio ads as part of the music business," and Figure 72 confirms it: **audio and video advertising totals $16.9 billion of the $43.0 billion denominator, roughly 39%.** Radio-station and web-delivered audio ads alone are $15.0 billion, about 35%. Musicians are not paid a performance royalty on U.S. terrestrial radio at all, so including that advertising revenue in the denominator mechanically depresses the artist share. Strip out all advertising and the same $5.0 billion to artists is about **19%** of the remaining $26.1 billion — a figure Citi does not report, and one this paper should compute for itself rather than borrow. See https://www.billboard.com/music/music-news/citi-report-music-biz-industry-artist-revenue-inconsistent-analysis-8469666/ and https://www.billboard.com/articles/business/8469860/citi-music-industry-report-dissected-analysis. **If the paper uses 12%, it must state the denominator.**

**Streaming per-play figures, verbatim** (Citi, quoting an industry interviewee, so treat as illustrative not measured): "even if you are a fully independent artist, you are likely to earn around $15,000-$20,000 per million plays on a streaming service and that gets split between the writers… And here it is important to remember that 99% of all plays on Spotify go to 10% of songs. This means 90% of artists get little or nothing. And if you are on an 'old industry' label, you can expect to only get $1,700 per million plays, because the label is taking the lion's share of the economics."

**The published critiques, in detail.** Both Billboard articles are now behind a paywall (HTTP 402); verbatim text was captured from the Internet Archive and saved locally as `news_2018-08-10_Billboard_music-biz-slams-citi-report-CherieHu.md` and `news_2018-08-13_Billboard_citi-report-dissected-RobertLevine.md`.

- **RIAA:** "it's unfortunate that some of the methodologies and analysis within this report are incorrect or incomplete… RIAA data was extensively cited, but none of our senior team was consulted."
- **A major-label source, on the denominator:** "Right off the bat, before you even look at what a label pays an artist, you're starting with a huge base of ad revenue that isn't going to anybody on the recorded music side, thanks to government regulation… That 12-percent artist share would go up dramatically if radio were out of the equation." The same source puts the radio figure at "almost $15 billion of the $43 billion" — which Figure 72 confirms exactly.
- **Robert Levine, Billboard:** "The bank arrives at its 12 percent number by counting revenue from radio ads as part of the music business, which is unusual. (Oddly, it doesn't seem to count revenue from merchandise sales.)" And: "Saying artists make 12 percent of industry revenue is like saying a bar that Jeff Bezos walks into is full of people with an average net worth of more than a billion dollars — it's completely beside the point." Levine also notes Citi does not net touring costs out of artist concert revenue: "income from royalties is pure profit for artists. That's not the case with their percentage of ticket grosses."
- **Kevin Erickson, Future of Music Coalition**, on the framing itself: "Is the percent of industry revenue that makes it to artists really a good measure of how well the industry is doing? If all working artists quit what they were doing and went out on the street and started busking, they would make 100 percent of that revenue, but that would be stupid. In isolation, that data point doesn't capture much."
- **Other specific errors alleged:** Citi implies ~$800M US YouTube music revenue when RIAA's 2017 figure for the entire ad-supported streaming category was $659M; RIAA notes "the analysts imply a per-stream royalty rate of $0.003 for YouTube, which is much higher than seen in other public reports." Citi assumes a 12–20% artist physical retail royalty (avg ~15%) against a label source's "closer to mid-20s in reality… streaming and subscriptions, the artist royalty is much closer to the mid-30s."
- **Disclosed conflict:** Citi counts SiriusXM and Live Nation as investment banking clients — firms the report itself names as consolidation drivers.
- **Bazinet's response:** "It's fair to say that averages can always be misleading… But we just tried to go right down the fairway, and our overall numbers are pretty close."

**Academic sources that WERE obtained** (so the paper is not without peer-reviewed grounding on musician earnings):

**Academic gap, partially closed — see the correction below:**

- **A UT Austin project on Austin musicians DOES exist**, and a sibling evidence file in this folder documents it in full: **"Support Austin Musicians," University of Texas at Austin, 2024**, associated with UT professor Eric Drott and the UT School of Music. https://supportaustinmusicians.org/final-summary/ | see `study_2024_UT-Austin_SupportAustinMusicians.md` in this folder.
