# Findings — Music Census & Pay Surveys

Evidence collected 2026-08-01. Every bullet names the document that supports it. Numbers are verbatim from primary sources unless marked otherwise.

**Read this first:** the single most consequential finding is #1. It constrains what the white paper can honestly claim about change over time.

---

## The evidence base itself

**1. There is no measurement of Austin musician earnings after 2013.** The 2022 Greater Austin Music Census deliberately dropped the income questions: it "removed questions from the 2014 survey that aimed to quantify the income of music people in a way that turned off many respondents" (2022 Summary Report, p.2). Every income figure in circulation — including in 2026 journalism — traces back to the 2014 census, which measured **pre-tax calendar 2013** income. Any claim that musician incomes have or have not changed since 2014 is unsupported by data. *Sources: `austin_music_census_2022_SUMMARY_REPORT.pdf` p.2; `austin_music_census_2014_vs_2022_extracted_numbers.md`.*

**2. The brief's attribution is wrong and should be corrected.** The 2014/2015 Austin Music Census was authored by **Titan Music Group, LLC** (principal Nikki Rowling), not TXP. TXP, Inc. produced the *separate* economic-impact series (2001, 2007, 2012, and the Feb 2016 update). Rowling later founded Sound Music Cities, which ran the 2022 census — which is why the same analyst can publish 2014-vs-2022 comparisons. *Source: `austin_music_census_2015_TitanMusicGroup_full.pdf`, title page.*

**3. Both censuses are self-selected online convenience samples, and response fell 43%.** n=3,968 (2014) → n=2,260 (2022). Neither supports population-level inference. The 2022 report frames its own comparisons as "directional shifts," not estimates. *Sources: both census PDFs.*

## What the 2014 census actually found

**4. 68.4% of Austin musicians earned under $10,000 from music in 2013** — 1,288 of 1,883 respondents. This is the strongest single number in the folder. From all sources including non-music jobs, 20.5% earned under $10,000 and roughly one-third lived on $15,000 or less. *Source: 2015 census, Figs. 1–2, pp. 23–24.*

**5. More than 20.5% of musician respondents fell below the 2014 federal poverty line of $11,670 — against an Austin MSA rate of 14.0%** — and the report notes "another 30% hover just above it." More than 50% qualified for Section 8 housing subsidies; ~75% earned below the MSA mean wage of $48,150; more than 62% below the median wage of $36,640. Nearly a third earned less than a full-time minimum-wage income ($15,080). *Source: 2015 census, Fig. 4, p. 25.*

**6. Only 22.7% of musician respondents were full-time musicians**; 56% held a non-music job, 15% a second music-industry job, and over 60% of all respondents worked two or more jobs — even though 54% had been working as musicians for more than ten years. *Source: 2015 census, p. 24.*

**7. Stagnating pay was already the loudest complaint in 2014, not a recent development. 87.3%** of musician respondents ranked "Stagnating Pay / Difficult to Make a Viable Income" as having extreme or strong impact — the highest-ranked factor in the entire survey. *Source: 2015 census, pp. 28–29.*

## What the 2022 census found

**8. 89% intend to keep working in music, but only 64% expect to still live in greater Austin in three years** — a 25-point gap the authors read as "a third of the ecosystem is considering... [moving] out of the metro area completely." **38% are struggling to afford rent or mortgage**, the top expense category, ahead of dental/vision insurance (28%) and health care (21%). *Source: 2022 Summary Report, p.7; Data Appendix p.71.*

**9. Gig frequency fell measurably. Musicians reporting more than 3 paid local gigs a month dropped from 43% (2014) to 35% (2022); those reporting zero rose from 7.6% to 14%.** Restricting to creatives who said their work had returned to pre-pandemic levels still leaves the >3-gig share at 38%, below 2014. Only **54% of gigs pay a base guarantee**. *Source: Data Appendix pp. 42–45.*

**10. Musicians are being pushed outward, unevenly. Average distance from downtown is 10.6 miles; median 8 miles.** But it is 12.9 miles for BIPOC respondents and 12.6 for the uninsured, against 6.6 for venue owners. 44% say housing costs rose in the past two years and **15% say housing costs caused a move**. *Source: Data Appendix pp. 6–7, 77.*

**11. Economic precarity sorts experience of the scene more sharply than race or gender.** In the DEI crosstabs, respondents with **no health insurance** are the most negative subgroup on nearly every dimension: 76% call the scene elitist (vs. 51% of homeowners), 62% ageist, 57% individualistic, 46% unsupportive, 45% unwelcoming. Renters run 10–16 points more negative than owners on every item. This is the closest thing to an income gradient the 2022 data contains. *Source: DEI Appendix pp. 9–15.*

**12. Costs are of the same order as music revenue for much of the population.** Average annual business spend by creatives is **$10,500** (new music $3,538, workspace $1,390, gear rentals $1,378, promotion $1,350), of which 60% ($6,300) is spent locally. Set against the 2014 finding that 68.4% earned under $10,000 from music, a large share of this workforce spends about what it earns. *Source: Data Appendix p.46. Cross-vintage comparison — flag as illustrative, not a matched calculation.*

**13. The safety net barely reaches this workforce. 61% received no COVID relief at all**, and among those who received it through a music job, **80% said it was the first government support they had ever received as a music professional**. Offsetting good news: 84% now have health insurance, up 5 points since 2014, which the report credits to HAAM. *Source: Data Appendix pp. 72–73; Summary Report p.10.*

**15. The pipeline is thinning.** Respondents with under three years in the industry fell from 14% to 6.6%, while those with 10+ years rose from 49.2% to 65.5%. The 25–39 age band fell from 49% to 38%; the 65+ band tripled from 3% to 9%. Sound Music Cities reads this as "fewer younger people are entering the industry." *Caveat: 2022 outreach ran through established community partners, which could inflate the tenured share.* *Source: Summary Report p.6.*

## The economics behind the "$1.8 billion" headline

**16. Austin's music sector grew to $1.83 billion in 2014 — but core music jobs fell while tourism jobs grew.** TXP found total output of **$1,831.7 million**, 20,723 jobs, and $47.13 million in City tax revenue in 2014, up from $1.6 billion and ~18,000 jobs in 2010. Decomposed: music-related **tourism added 3,780 jobs (+37%)** while **music and its secondary effects lost 1,205 jobs**, leaving over two-thirds of all jobs on the tourism side. The "booming industry, broke musicians" framing is supported by the industry's own impact study. *Source: `TXP_austin_music_econ_impact_update_2016.pdf` pp. 3–4.*

## Pay norms and the claim to avoid

**17. The "$150 a gig, unchanged since the 1980s" claim does not survive contact with its sources.** It merges three separate things:
- **Market rate:** KUT (Feb 15, 2022) documents **$50–$100 per gig per musician**, flat for 40+ years — evidenced by Marcia Ball's 1979 calendar ($300–$600/night for a five-piece = $60–$120 each) and drummer David Sanger's 1994 records ($75, $80, $100).
- **City policy rate:** **$150 per musician per hour** was the City of Austin's own booking rate from 2016, raised to **$200** in March 2022. As of 2026 it is tiered: $200 (groups ≤6), $150 (7–10), $1,500 (10+).
- **Units differ:** $150/$200 are *per hour*; $50–$100 are *per gig*.
**Recommended formulation:** market pay per musician per gig has been roughly $50–$100 for four decades per KUT's reporting, while the City's own booking floor rose from $150 (2016) to $200 (2022). *Sources: `news_2022-02-15_KUT_*`, `news_2022-03-17_KUT_*`, `news_2026-07-16_AustinChronicle_*`.*

**18. The $15,475 figure is not a median — it is one sideman's 1981 salary.** It comes from Marcia Ball's 1981 calendar: what her saxophone player earned working a full year for her. KUT used it rhetorically ("many Austin musicians are still only making $15,475 a year") and noted it would be **$47,865** in 2022 dollars — which is the actual point. **Do not cite $15,475 as an Austin musician median income.** *Source: `news_2022-02-15_KUT_musician-pay-unchanged-40-years.md`.*

**19. SXSW artist pay, documented time series.** Solo/band per showcase: **$100/$250** pre-2023 (unchanged "for at least a decade") → **$150/$350** (SXSW 2024) → **$175/$400** (SXSW 2025). Application fee rose from $55 (2023) to $75 (2024). UMAW demands a $750 minimum and estimates SXSW collected ~$385,495 in application fees against ~$57,820 paid to artists — *UMAW's own estimate, not audited; label it as such.* In 2024 roughly 120 artists withdrew over Army/defense sponsorship, which SXSW subsequently dropped. *Sources: `news_2024_UMAW_*`, `news_2023-03-16_NPR_*`, `news_2024-06-26_KUT_*`.*

## The nonprofit safety net

**20. HAAM's membership is poorer than the census population, and HAAM does not publish a true median.** Its published statistics are share-below-threshold and the threshold moves annually. Best available proxies: **2024 — "half of HAAM musicians make less than $1,975 a month"** (~$23,700/yr); **2025 — half make less than $24,000/yr, 75% less than $31,000**; 2022 — 50% under $1,750/mo, 57% earning "barely enough or not enough to get by," 34% worried about affording food. **HAAM publishes no share below the federal poverty line** — the only FPL reference is a 400%-FPL *eligibility ceiling*, which must not be converted into a poverty rate. *Source: `nonprofit_ecosystem_numbers.md` §1a.*

**21. HAAM has scaled enormously: cumulative healthcare value delivered went $46M (2015) → $73.7M (2019) → $123M (2022) → $215M (2025)**, with annual service value rising from $115,000 in 2005 to over $19 million in 2025. Membership grew 450 (2005) → 3,136 (2022) → 3,149 (2023). **The sharpest COVID statistic in the whole corpus: HAAM member food insecurity went from 29% pre-COVID to 74% post-COVID**, with 84% needing housing help. *Source: `nonprofit_ecosystem_numbers.md` §1b–1c; 2020 HAAM report.*

**22. SIMS and Black Fret are small relative to need.** SIMS FY2023 IRS Form 990 Schedule I: **711 recipients, $536,205 in grants** — the only filing-grade annual count available. Cumulative since 1995: 25,000 clients, 300,000 sessions, $50M. In FY2020 SIMS revenue collapsed to $766,850 against $1.14M in expenses as demand rose. Black Fret (now **Sonic Guild**) reports over $7.3M to 500+ musicians since 2013, but that is **Austin and Seattle combined**; Austin awards fell from **$252,000 to 20 artists (2021) to $150,000 to 20 artists (2025)** even as reported revenue rose. *Source: `nonprofit_ecosystem_numbers.md` §2–3.*

**23. The City's Live Music Fund shows a real policy tradeoff, verified line-by-line from the City's own awardee lists.** FY23: **$3,490,000 across 369 awards** (329 × $10,000 + 40 × $5,000). FY24: **$4,365,000 across 136 awards** (104 × $30,000 + 17 × $60,000 + 15 × $15,000). More money reached less than half as many recipients; average award rose from ~$9,460 to ~$32,100 and the acceptance rate fell from 56% (368/660) to under 14% (137/1,000+). FY25 made no awards; a 2025 "reset" cut maximum award size to spread money wider. In June 2025, 60 grantees were non-compliant with $847,500 unpaid, $550,000 of it Live Music Fund. *Sources: `austin_live_music_fund_FY23/FY24_awardees.pdf` (independently re-tallied — note the City PDFs yield 369/136 against KUT's 368/137, a one-record discrepancy worth resolving); `news_2025-10-21_KUT_*`; `news_2025-06-19_AustinMonitor_*`.*

## National and cross-city benchmarks

**24. Austin's musicians are not obviously worse off than peers — but nobody can prove it either way, because the instruments differ.** Average annual music income from the 2024 Sound Music Cities censuses: **Nashville $52,000, Baltimore $35,000, Minneapolis $28,000, New Orleans $23,000, Washington DC $23,000, Cleveland $21,000.** Washington DC is the only city publishing a **median local gig rate: $150** (median touring gig $100) — a direct and useful comparator for the Austin per-gig debate. Average local gig: Nashville $333.55, New Orleans $536, DC $446, Cleveland $307, Baltimore $309. **These are averages, not medians, and none uses Austin's 2014 instrument** — do not splice them onto an Austin series. *Source: `national_and_benchmark_surveys.md` §6.*

**24b. The one true median available for another city closely matches Austin's 2014 result.** Sound Music Cities' 2019 Charlotte ecosystem study reports that **"Median income from music is less than $10,000, 80% work as freelancers"** (n=1,802; 766 professionals). Austin's 2014 finding was that 68.4% earned under $10,000 from music. Two different cities, five years apart, both land at roughly "the typical working musician earns under $10,000 a year from music." That convergence is the strongest available evidence that Austin's numbers reflect a structural feature of the profession rather than something peculiar to Austin — which is exactly the comparison the 2015 census said it could not make ("there are simply no parallel data with which to compare the findings"). *Source: `census_charlotte_2019_ecosystem_study.pdf` (recovered from a truncated download; 84 pp.).*

**25. The national MIRA benchmark: median music-related income of $21,300 in 2017**, with median *total* income near $35,000 and 61% saying music income alone was insufficient to meet living expenses. **Citation warning:** press coverage routinely conflates three numbers — the ACS figure for all U.S. musicians' total personal income ($20,000–$25,000), MIRA's median total income (~$35,000), and MIRA's median music income ($21,300). Cite the report, not the coverage. Sample is nonrandom, n=1,227, 62% recruited from MusiCares clients. *Source: `mira_musician_survey_2018.pdf`; `national_and_benchmark_surveys.md` §1.*

## Five most striking numbers

1. **68.4%** of Austin musicians earned under **$10,000** from music in 2013 (2015 census, n=1,883).
2. Between 2010 and 2014 Austin's music sector added **3,780 tourism jobs (+37%)** while **losing 1,205 music jobs** — inside the same "$1.8 billion industry" headline (TXP 2016).
3. **89%** of 2022 respondents plan to keep working in music; only **64%** plan to still live in greater Austin in three years (2022 census).
4. HAAM member food insecurity went from **29% pre-COVID to 74% post-COVID** (HAAM 2020 annual report).
5. **$15,475** — the figure quoted for years as an Austin musician's annual income — is one saxophone player's **1981** salary from a bandleader's personal calendar, worth **$47,865** in 2022 dollars (KUT, Feb 2022).

## Gaps and blockers

- **No post-2013 Austin income measurement exists.** This is the central evidence gap. Options: commission a question set, obtain HAAM's underlying member survey, or use PUMS/OEWS from the sibling evidence folders as the quantitative spine and treat the censuses as qualitative.
- **No Austin census after 2022.** Austin appears in Sound Music Cities' 2023–24 cohort roster but published no report. **Worth a direct inquiry** — if Austin data exists it is the most current evidence available.
- **HAAM publishes no survey methodology** — no n, response rate, or field dates in any report 2014–2025. Direct request needed before citing HAAM income figures as measurements.
- **Two KVUE articles could not be retrieved** (Akamai 403); saved as stubs with **unverified dates**. The Council $200 story matters — it would settle whether $200 is a binding Council minimum or departmental policy.
- **Americans for the Arts COVID figures are secondary only** (via ARTnews). The $22,000 / $50.6 billion / 63% variants conflict with the verified April 2020 figures and are flagged do-not-publish.
- **Not completed** (WebSearch budget exhausted at 200 calls): Austin Music Disaster Relief Fund, Banding Together ATX, Red River relief totals, Texas Music Office grants, Austin Community Foundation music funds, MusiCares Texas breakout.
- **Seven cross-source discrepancies** are logged in the journalism index files, including the 10% vs 12% central-Austin decline and a KUT "45 gigs a month" figure whose arithmetic does not reconcile. Resolve before publication.
- **One-record discrepancy** between the City's Live Music Fund awardee PDFs (369/136) and KUT's reporting (368/137).
