# Findings — Creative Economy / BEA ACPSA / NASAA / NEA
Retrieval date: 2026-08-01. All BEA figures are nominal (current dollars) unless noted "real."

1. **Texas arts/culture value added reached $65.6 billion in 2023 (2.5% of state GDP)** — up from
   $27.4 billion (3.5% of state GDP) in 2001, a nominal CAGR of ~4.0%/year. Employment was 360,964
   jobs and compensation was $32.0 billion (source: `acpsa_state_totals_long_2001_2023.csv`).

2. **Texas's arts/culture GDP share is the lowest of the 8 benchmark states and below the US average.**
   2023 ranking: Washington 9.8%, New York 7.6%, California 7.5%, US average 4.2%, Tennessee 4.0%,
   Georgia 3.8%, Florida 3.2%, **Texas 2.5%**, Louisiana 2.1%. Among all 50 states + DC, Texas ranks
   **36th** (Washington is 1st at 9.8%; Delaware is last at 1.2%).

3. **Texas's arts/culture value added has consistently grown more slowly than California's and New
   York's since 2001.** 2001→2023 nominal CAGR: California 5.3%/yr, New York 4.8%/yr, Tennessee
   4.8%/yr, US 4.2%/yr, Texas 4.0%/yr. Texas is growing roughly in line with the national average but
   trailing the two largest peer economies.

4. **Within Texas, "independent artists, writers, and performers" has overtaken "performing arts
   companies" as the larger value-added category** — a proxy for the shift from institutional/employee
   arts work toward gig/freelance creative work. The independent-artist category was smaller than
   performing-arts-companies from 2001–2010 (e.g., $230M vs. $374M in 2001) but has been larger in
   most years since 2011, reaching $1.64B vs. $876M in 2023 — a gap of roughly $767 million, the widest
   in the series (peaked at $1.02B gap in 2022). This is directly relevant to a "working musicians"
   narrative: the data show TX's growth is concentrated in solo/independent creative labor, not
   traditional arts institutions.

5. **Sound recording (the industry closest to the recorded-music business) shows a U-shaped national
   trend: national nominal value added fell from $18.0B (2001) to a low of $13.3B (2019), then jumped
   to $21.1B (2023)** — consistent with the well-documented early-2000s/2010s disruption (piracy,
   then low per-stream royalties) followed by a post-pandemic streaming-revenue recovery. In real
   (chained 2017$) terms the industry still grew faster than the ACPSA-wide average over the full
   1998–2023 window (3.96%/yr vs. 3.17%/yr for all ACPSA industries), but that headline CAGR masks a
   ~15-year trough. (Source: `acpsa_national_industry_detail_nominal_2001_2023.csv`,
   `acpsa_national_real_value_added_industry_1998_2023.csv`.)

6. **Nationally, "performing arts companies" real value added has been essentially flat for 25 years**
   (chained-2017$ CAGR 1998–2023 = +0.63%/yr) while "independent artists, writers, and performers"
   grew at 2.20%/yr over the same window — the same institutional-to-independent shift visible in the
   Texas state data (#4) shows up in the national industry aggregates too.

7. **Texas ranks near the bottom nationally in per-capita state arts-agency funding, though it moved
   up somewhat in FY2026.** NASAA's FY2026 per-capita table (Table 6) ranks Texas **39th of 50 states**
   on legislative appropriation including line items ($0.58/capita) and **44th of 50** on total agency
   revenue per capita ($0.65/capita) — up from **46th** and **48th**, respectively, in FY2025. On
   appropriation as a share of each state's general-fund budget, Texas ranks **35th of 49** (0.019% of
   a $95.9 billion general fund) — tied with Washington and West Virginia. All three metrics place
   Texas solidly in the bottom third nationally, though not in the single-digit-worst tier on every cut.

8. **Texas Commission on the Arts' state appropriation jumped 27.7% from FY2025 to FY2026** —
   from $14.32 million to $18.29 million in legislative appropriation (matching almost exactly between
   NASAA's independently-surveyed figure and TCA's own operating-budget documents: $14,320,385 →
   $18,289,600). Including federal funds and other state sources, TCA's total operating budget rose
   from $18.06 million (FY2025) to $20.05 million (FY2026). The 2026–27 biennium's total state funding
   rose to roughly $39.8 million, a $5.7 million increase over the prior biennium against an original
   $11 million request (KERA News, 2025-05-30) — i.e., TCA received about half of what it asked for.

9. **Nationally, state arts-agency appropriations declined in FY2026 even as Texas's rose.** SAAs
   collectively received $646.0 million in FY2026 legislative appropriations (incl. line items), a
   5.9% decrease from FY2025's $694.3 million — driven largely by a swing in one-time "line item"
   funding ($128.3M → $73.1M). Texas moved counter to the national trend, one of 25 states reporting
   an increase (national median increase among those 25 was 6.6%; Texas's 27.7% increase was well
   above that median).

10. **Federal funding to Texas arts organizations is contracting at the same time state funding is
    rising.** The NEA Texas Fact Sheet (2026) shows $42.5 million distributed to Texas over the past
    five years, but KERA News (2025-05-30) reports Texas arts groups have already lost over $1 million
    in NEA support, with continued uncertainty over TCA's roughly $1.3–1.5 million/year NEA allocation
    — a relevant federal-funding-risk caveat for the white paper.

11. **The BEA Arts and Cultural Production Satellite Account — the single best source of state-level
    arts/culture GDP, employment, and compensation data — appears to have been discontinued as a
    regular annual release.** BEA/NEA posted a February 2026 notice that BEA will no longer regularly
    produce ACPSA statistics; the April 2025 release (2023 data) is therefore the most recent and
    likely final installment as of this writing. Any recommendation in the white paper that depends on
    future ACPSA updates should flag this data-continuity risk.

12. **A minor rounding discrepancy exists between the precise BEA ratio and NEA/TCA's public-facing
    rounding of Texas's arts GDP share:** BEA's own ratio table computes exactly 2.5% for 2023
    ($65,569,985K ÷ $2,583,866,227K), while the NEA Texas Fact Sheet and related outreach materials
    round this to "3 percent." Both derive from the same underlying release; use 2.5% for precision
    and attribute "~3%" only when directly quoting NEA/TCA messaging.

13. **Washington state's unusually high arts/culture GDP share (9.8%, #1 nationally) is a useful
    cautionary note for peer-state comparisons** — it likely reflects a heavy weighting toward
    "design services" (software/UX/industrial design, tied to WA's large tech sector) rather than
    performing arts or music specifically, since ACPSA's "core arts and cultural production" bucket
    bundles design services with music/performing-arts/museums. Any cross-state VA-share comparison
    in the white paper should note this industry-mix caveat rather than reading WA's #1 rank as
    evidence of unusually strong music/performing-arts activity.

14. **Independent artists' employment in Texas grew faster than value added would suggest is
    "stable," with volatility around 2020.** TX independent-artist jobs fell from 7,111 (2019) to
    4,065 (2020) — a 43% drop — before recovering to 6,610 by 2023, still below the 2019 peak. This
    is a useful, if indirect, indicator of pandemic-era income disruption for Texas's independent
    creative workforce (performing-arts-company jobs saw a similar, slightly smaller, 29% drop in 2020).
