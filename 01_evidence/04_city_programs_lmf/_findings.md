# Findings — City of Austin music programs and money

Evidence notes only. No interpretation beyond what the documents state.
Compiled 2026-08-01. Source for each bullet is in `_sources.md`.
Tier markers: **[P]** primary city document or city open data · **[S]** secondary reporting · **[D]** derived by me from a primary source.

## How the money is generated

1. **[P] The Live Music Fund is a fixed 15% slice of a 2-percentage-point hotel tax increase, not an appropriation Council sets each year.** Ordinance No. 20190919-149 (adopted 2019-09-19, effective 2019-09-30) raised Austin's Hotel Occupancy Tax from 7% to 9% and directed "an amount equal to 15% of the two percent assessment" to the Live Music Fund, another 15% to Historic Preservation, and the balance to Convention Center expansion. PART 4 reads simply: "Council directs the City Manager to establish the Live Music Fund." Because the share is fixed in code, fund revenue tracks hotel demand rather than policy choices about musicians.

2. **[P] Live Music Fund revenue grew from $1.49M to roughly $4.8M and has now plateaued.** Actual fund revenue by fiscal year: FY2021 **$1,485,269**; FY2022 **$3,699,512**; FY2023 **$4,665,105**; FY2024 **$4,816,545**; FY2025 **$4,773,754**; FY2026 **$4,145,514** (partial-year actual against a $4,812,407 budget and a $4,990,026 current-year estimate). FY2027 is budgeted at **$5,051,453**. The fund has been flat in nominal terms since FY2024, meaning it has lost real purchasing power over three years.

3. **[P] The Live Music Fund is a small share of Austin's hotel-tax machine.** Total Hotel Occupancy Tax Fund revenue was **$164.6M in FY2025** and is budgeted at **$167.3M for FY2026**. Against that, the Live Music Fund's ~$4.8M is roughly **2.9%** of city HOT revenue. For comparison in the same year, the Cultural Arts Fund drew **$16.4M** and the Historic Preservation Fund **$21.1M** — that is, historic preservation receives about **4.4 times** what live music receives.

## What was actually disbursed

4. **[P] The fund collected money for more than three years before paying a single meaningful grant.** Expenditures were **$4,284 in FY2021** and **$50,000 in FY2022** against revenue of $1.49M and $3.70M in those years. The first real grant cycle did not disburse until FY2023 (**$4,089,031**). **[D]** By the close of FY2022 the fund had accumulated roughly **$5.13M** in unspent cash while musicians were in the depths of pandemic-era income loss.

5. **[P] Cumulative Live Music Fund spending FY2021–FY2026 is $20,204,747** against cumulative revenue of **$23,585,699**, leaving a **[D] $3,380,951** cumulative gap. Annual expenditures: FY2021 $4,284 · FY2022 $50,000 · FY2023 $4,089,031 · FY2024 $5,148,606 · FY2025 $5,424,210 · FY2026 $5,488,616. Since FY2024 the fund has spent more than it took in, drawing the accumulated surplus down. *(Caveat: this is a cash-flow proxy from FY2021 forward, not an audited fund balance; it excludes any FY2020 opening balance. Press reporting put the balance at ~$3.2M in September 2025 against my $4.72M cumulative figure for the same point — the two do not reconcile and the ACFR schedule is needed.)*

6. **[P] About 13 cents of every Live Music Fund dollar spent has gone to something other than grants.** Cumulative expenditure by object, FY2021–FY2026: **Grants to subrecipients $17,598,450 (87.1%)**; **Consultant-others $2,338,304 (11.6%)** — the third-party administrator; **Advertising/publication $267,993 (1.3%)**. In FY2026 alone the budget carried **$675,000** for consultants and **$100,000** for advertising against **$4,800,000** in grants.

## Cohorts, award sizes, and who gets in

7. **[P] Award sizes have swung wildly across four cycles, and the number of musicians funded moved inversely.** FY2023: **369 awards, $3,490,000** (40 at $5,000; 329 at $10,000) — average **[D] $9,458**. FY2024: **136 awards, $4,365,000** (15 at $15,000; 104 at $30,000; 17 venues at $60,000) — average **[D] $32,096**, but the number of funded musicians and promoters collapsed from ~369 to 119. FY2026: **399 awards, $7,140,000** — average **[D] $17,895**. A musician's expected grant depended heavily on which year they applied.

8. **[P] FY2024 admitted live music venues to the fund for the first time**, at $30,000 for venues with operating budgets under $100,000 and $60,000 for those above. By FY2026 the venue tier had risen to a flat **$70,000** and **20 venues** took **$1,400,000** — about **19.6%** of the LMF total. The FY2026 venue list includes Antone's, Mohawk, Elysium, Flamingo Cantina, Hotel Vegas, Sahara Lounge, Sam's Town Point, Valhalla, and The Concourse Project.

9. **[S] Applicant success rates fell sharply when award sizes rose.** FY2023: **660 applicants, 368 awards** — roughly **56%** funded. FY2024: **1,013 applicants requesting $23M, 120 artists selected** — roughly **13%** funded. **[P]** For the whole FY2026 ACME cycle across all programs, **1,606 applicants requested over $67M** and **731 grants totaling $24M+** were made — about **46%** of applicants funded and about **36%** of requested dollars supplied. No published applicant count exists for the FY2026 Live Music Fund alone.

10. **[P] There was no FY2025 cohort.** The official program page reports **zero FY25 awards**; the cycle slipped and applications moved to fall 2025. **[S]** The planned FY25 design had been $4.8M across 60 grants of $5,000, 175 of $20,000, and 17 venue grants of $60,000. This is a full year in which musicians could not access the fund, and it likely explains the FY2026 jump to $7.14M.

11. **[P] The current FY27 cycle is a $6.0M program**, down from $7.14M in FY26, with applications open 2026-07-07 to 2026-08-18 and tiers of **$5,000** (12-month), **$20,000** (24-month), and **$70,000** for venues. It is administered by **the Long Center for the Performing Arts** as third-party administrator, with applications routed through the Long Center's Submittable portal rather than the City.

12. **[P] The FY2026 award list does not reconcile with itself.** It states "399 awards totaling $7,140,000.00," but the venue section names **21** venues while its own subtotal says **20** venues / $1,400,000, and the musician/promoter section claims **377 funded / $5,740,000** while the itemized rows sum to **$5,650,000** — a **$90,000** gap. Cite the document's stated totals and flag the discrepancy; do not present line-level tallies as authoritative.

## Compliance and oversight

13. **[S] A large share of Live Music Fund grantees were out of compliance in mid-2025.** Data presented to the Austin Arts Commission on 2025-06-16 showed **60 Live Music Fund recipients** non-compliant with reporting requirements, freezing **$550,000** in hotel-tax money (of **$847,500** frozen citywide across 70 contracts), with a further **$70,000** in LMF awards fully forfeited after recipients declined or did not respond. Recipients must file final reports to receive their last 10% and to stay eligible for future cycles. ACME's assistant director attributed part of the problem to musicians not regularly checking email.

14. **[P] No audit of the Live Music Fund exists, and the one arts-funding audit that came close deliberately excluded music.** The Office of the City Auditor's "Special Request: EDD Culture and Arts Funding" (October 2024), requested by five Council members, states in its scope: "we did not look at funding for music or music disaster" relief. A search of the auditor's full report index returns **zero** music-titled audits. The 2024 audit did find that cultural arts administrative costs ran **4.9% to 8.4%** of total expenditures FY2018–FY2023, and that third-party administrators of ARPA and CARES money took a **4.5%** fee.

## Other city programs for musicians and creative space

15. **[P] The Iconic Venue Fund is real, is roughly the size of a third of the Live Music Fund, and is fully spent.** It drew steady revenue of about **$2.4M–$2.55M** per year FY2021–FY2025 and has paid out **$15,300,000** in grants to subrecipients cumulatively. FY2026 spending was **$2,900,000 against only $4,614 in recorded revenue**, pushing cumulative revenue-minus-expenditure to **[D] −$2,593,098** — the fund is drawing down. Note FY2022 saw **$0** spent despite $2.52M collected.

16. **[P] Creative Space Assistance Program totals by cycle: FY19 19 awards / $590,874** (range $6,248–$50,000, average **[D] $31,099**); **FY23 64 awards / $1,481,126** (range $5,000–$50,000, average **[D] $23,143**); **FY26 22 awards / $1,320,000** (flat $60,000 each). The FY26 design trades breadth for size — a third as many recipients as FY23 at 2.6 times the average award. Award lists for FY20–FY22, FY24 and FY25 are not published.

17. **[P] Pandemic-era creative space relief was heavily oversubscribed.** The 2020 Austin Creative Space Disaster Relief round distributed **$987,943 to 32 of 65 applicants**, with **$3.7M requested against $1M available** — roughly **3.7 times** oversubscribed. Recipients were 12 live music venues, 11 arts nonprofits, 7 theaters/performance venues, 1 museum/gallery, and 1 individual artist.

18. **[P] The Music Venue Assistance Program Fund is effectively dead.** It records only **$98 (FY2021)** and **$225 (FY2022)** in revenue, and its expenditures are administrative wind-down: **$42,813** transferred to Economic Development, **$34,725** in bad debt expense, and small transfers out. Nothing after FY2023.

19. **[S] COVID-era music relief was modest and thinly documented.** Ordinance 20200423-067 (2020-04-23) designated **$1.5M** for the Austin Music Disaster Relief Fund at **$1,000 per musician**, restricted to musicians with two months or less of living expenses on hand and distributed first-come, first-served through MusiCares beginning 2020-06-08. Separately, Council created the **$15M SAVES Fund** (Ordinance 20201001-052) including a **$5M** Live Music Venue Preservation Fund offering up to $20,000 immediately plus monthly grants up to $40,000 to a **$140,000** cap, administered by the Long Center. **Final recipient counts and disbursed totals for the disaster relief fund were not found in any city document.**

## Pay standards and the built environment

20. **[P] Austin sets a musician pay floor, but only for performances the City itself commissions.** Resolution No. 20230720-123 (2023-07-20) establishes **$200 per musician** for groups up to six, **$150 per musician** for groups of seven to ten, and **$1,500 total** for groups of ten or more. The resolution recites that the prior prevailing rate was **$100/hour set in 2012** and **$150/hour in 2016** — meaning the rate was unchanged for seven years before this. It binds "all City departments directly hiring" musicians and asks the Music Commission to review it regularly; it does not reach SXSW or private promoters. **[S]** The Music Commission voted 7-0 in August 2023 asking Council to require fair pay in SXSW negotiations for city resources; no council resolution imposing that was located. SXSW independently raised 2024 rates to **$350 for bands and $150 for solo artists**, up $100 and $50 respectively.

21. **[S] The 2017 Red River extended-hours pilot reported gains for musicians specifically.** Five venues (Stubb's, Mohawk, Empire Control Room & Garage, Beerland, Cheer Up Charlies) ran later outdoor music hours from May to November 2017. Reported results: **+22% paid to local musicians**, **+10% local and regional acts booked**, **+5% paid to venue staff**, **+3% gross ticket sales** — against **−13% total tickets sold**. The pay gains came alongside falling attendance, which is worth probing. The evaluation memo itself was not located; only the draft implementing ordinance (Chapter 9-2 amendment, 2018-03-30) is in hand.

22. **[S] The "Agent of Change" ordinance has been debated for roughly a decade without a confirmed adoption.** The proposal would require developers building within **600 feet** of a venue holding an outdoor music permit to conduct sound tests during live performances and disclose findings in purchase or lease agreements, placing the soundproofing burden on the developer rather than the venue. Traced from a 2015 East Sixth Street venue dispute with the Westin Austin Downtown through a September 2024 Council agenda item. **Final status unresolved** — this needs to be confirmed before publication.

## Departmental context

23. **[P] For FY2026, the ACME department budget is $71,193,853**, split across Historic Preservation Fund **$31,379,499**; Cultural Arts Fund **$17,000,000**; General Fund **$11,392,925**; Live Music Fund **$5,575,000**; Economic Development Fund **$2,946,429**; Iconic Venue Fund **$2,900,000**. The **Music and Entertainment** activity lines specifically total about **$10.26M** across four funds, of which only **$1.78M** is non-grant operating money (General Fund $643,416 plus Economic Development Fund $1,140,061). Through Q3 of FY2026 the Live Music Fund had spent only **$981,616 of $5,575,000 (17.6%)**, while the Iconic Venue Fund had spent **100%** of its $2.9M.

24. **[P] Institutional home has moved twice, which breaks any budget time series.** Music programs sat in the Economic Development Department; the Office of Arts, Culture, Music and Entertainment (ACME) was established **2025-02-24**, consolidating cultural programming from five departments under Director Angela Means. Only FY2026 department-level detail exists in open data, so a Music & Entertainment Division budget-and-headcount trend cannot be built without pulling the printed Approved Budget volumes for FY2019–FY2025.

25. **[P] Long-run city cultural funding, for context.** The Cultural Funding Awards dataset records 6,476 individual awards from 1982 to FY2023. Totals rose from **$2.64M across 192 awards in FY2005** to a pre-pandemic peak of **$11.51M across 401 awards in FY2019**, collapsed to **$5.75M in FY2021**, and recovered to **$10.43M across 288 awards in FY2023**. This series covers the Cultural Arts Fund, **not** the Live Music Fund, and is missing FY2022 entirely with nothing after FY2023.
