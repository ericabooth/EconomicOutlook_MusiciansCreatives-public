# Findings — state music-economy policy benchmark

Beat: state-level music policy, Texas vs. peer states. Evidence gathered 2026-08-01.
Full documentation in `_sources.md` and the per-state `_notes_*.md` files.

---

## Two corrections to the tasking, before anything else

**1. The Texas Music Incubator Rebate Program was created by SB 609 (87th Legislature, 2021), not HB 2806 (86th, 2019).** HB 2806 carried the identical caption and passed the House on April 24, 2019, but its last action was "placed on the Senate intent calendar" on May 22, 2019. It died without a Senate floor vote. Cite Gov't Code ch. 485, Subchapter C, added by SB 609, effective September 1, 2021.

**2. New York's NYC Broadway credit is Tax Law § 24-c, not § 24-a.** Section 24-a is the separate, much smaller *upstate* Empire State Musical and Theatrical Production Credit ($8M/year). The two are routinely conflated, including by ESD's own report titles — the document ESD publishes as the "Musical and Theatrical Tax Credit Annual Report" is the upstate report, not the Broadway one.

## Texas: the numbers

1. **TMIR is funded by a permanent statutory tax diversion, not an annual appropriation fight.** SB 609 amended Tax Code § 183.023(c) to route **$10,000,000/year** of mixed beverage gross receipts tax and § 151.801(f) to route **$100,000/year** of sales tax on beer and wine into the Texas Music Incubator Account No. 5193. Total **$10,100,000/year, $20,200,000/biennium**. The rebate is literally a partial refund of the alcohol taxes the venues themselves remitted.

2. **Enacted 2021, funded 2023 — a two-year dead letter.** SB 609 § 4 ordered the office to open applications by September 1, 2022. SB 609 § 5 conditioned that on a specific appropriation. **The 2022-23 GAA (SB 1, 87R) contains no Texas Music Incubator appropriation** — verified by full-text search of the enacted act. The first appropriation is Rider 39 of the 2024-25 GAA (HB 1, 88R). The first application window opened **September 1, 2023**, a year late.

3. **FY2026 awards: $10.1 million to 180 recipients across 60 Texas cities** (Texas Music Office, gov.texas.gov/music/tmir). Average award **$56,111**. The full annual dedication was drawn down.

4. **Round one: 241 applications, 156 recipients** (64.7% approval). Reported average award $53,000 and reported total "approximately $10 million" — **these two figures are arithmetically inconsistent** (156 × $53,000 = $8.27M). Treat the round-one total as unverified; the defensible statement is the 156-of-241 ratio. Source is a single local news article (Denton Record-Chronicle, 11/7/2024), not an agency release.

5. **Award ceiling $100,000** per recipient per fiscal year, one per tax ID, capped at the lesser of $100,000 or the alcohol tax the applicant remitted the prior fiscal year.

6. **Eligibility is narrow in three specific ways.** Venues need a dedicated capacity of **no more than 3,000**. Festival promoters qualify **only in counties under 100,000 population** — which excludes every festival in Harris, Dallas, Tarrant, Bexar, Travis, Collin, Denton, El Paso, Hidalgo, Fort Bend, and Williamson counties, including Austin's. Applicants need **two years** of qualifying operating history, which excludes new venues. Plus five of eight operational criteria, one of which must be marketing live music through listings or hosting live music **five or more nights per week**.

7. **The rebate scales with alcohol sales, not with artist pay.** The statute requires that a venue have *a* written artist contract specifying compensation. It sets no threshold for how much artists are paid, and the award size is determined entirely by alcohol tax remitted. A well-paying venue with modest bar volume gets less than a high-volume bar with a stage.

8. **No public recipient list and no evaluation requirement.** The 2026-27 GAA Rider 40 requires the Governor's office to report film-incentive (TMIIIP) awards to the LBB semi-annually with "a listing of entities that were awarded funding, the date the award was announced, and the amount awarded to each recipient." **No parallel reporting rider exists for TMIR**, and Gov't Code ch. 485 Subchapter C imposes no publication or evaluation duty. Recipient-level data would require a Public Information Act request.

9. **Unspent money reverts.** Gov't Code § 485.046(d): at the end of each biennium, unobligated and unexpended account balances transfer back to general revenue. No multi-year smoothing.

10. **Within the same office, film outguns music roughly 15 to 1.** SB 22 (89R, 2025) created a Texas moving image industry incentive fund and directed the comptroller to deposit **$300 million per biennium** into it (new Tax Code § 151.801(g)), sunsetting September 1, 2035. Music's dedication over the same biennium is **$20.2 million**. Both programs sit in Gov't Code ch. 485 and are administered by the same Music, Film, Television, and Multimedia Office. Ratio **14.9 : 1**. The 2026-27 GAA also carries a **$30 million Higher Education Film Workforce Pilot Program** (Rider 45) with no music counterpart.

11. **The Texas Music Office has no line item of its own.** Its operating budget is bundled with the Texas Film Commission and the film incentive inside GAA Strategy C.2.1. For 2026-27 that strategy holds **$55,757,969 All Funds**; subtract the $20.2M music rebate and the $30M film workforce pilot and roughly **$5.56 million** remains for two years of Film Commission plus Music Office plus TMIIIP administration combined. The music share of that residual is not publicly identifiable.

12. **Music Friendly Texas: 92 certified communities, and zero dollars attached.** Certification requires a city-employed liaison, an advisory board, a 30–45 minute virtual workshop, an MOU, and registration in the Texas Music Industry Database. It carries no grant, no tax benefit, and no entitlement. Growth from 59 communities (~2023) to 92 (2025). No published evaluation of its effect on venues, employment, or venue survival. *(The page's headline count of 92 and its own transcribed list of 94 entries disagree — verify before publishing a precise number.)*

13. **Hotel occupancy tax: cities fund music through the general arts paragraph, Tax Code § 351.101(a)(4)**, which covers "the encouragement, promotion, improvement, and application of the arts, including instrumental and vocal music…". There is **no music-specific subsection**. Every dollar must also satisfy the chapeau's requirement that HOT revenue "be used only to promote tourism and the convention and hotel industry." Austin additionally leans on (a)(1), (a)(5), and (b). *(§ 351.103's allocation caps were not pulled — open item.)*

14. **Texas Commission on the Arts FY2026 appropriation: $18,288,573**, up 27.7% from $14,319,358 in FY2025 — the largest percentage increase among the five benchmark states. Per capita **$0.58, rank 39 of 50**; **0.019%** of state general fund expenditures. TCA publishes no discipline-level grant breakout, so **the music share of TCA grants is unavailable.**

15. **The most-cited Texas music number has methodology problems worth disclosing.** TXP, Inc.'s "The 2024 Economic Impact of Music in Texas" (January 2025, commissioned by TMO) reports **195,979 total jobs, $10.484B earnings, $31.655B economic activity, $564M state tax revenue**, and a direct footprint of 86,832 jobs and $4.881B earnings. But: job counts are built from **TMO's own self-listed Texas Music Directory**, a marketing asset of the agency being measured; revenue ratios are anchored to the **2017 Economic Census**; and **2024 is the first year music tourism was included**, so the apparent jump from the 2023 study (192,000 jobs, $26.6B, ~$470M) is partly a definitional change. TXP itself writes that direct music employment "has stagnated."

## Peer states: what the money actually did

16. **Georgia enacted a $15-million-a-year music tax credit and paid out exactly zero dollars.** The Georgia Department of Audits and Accounts evaluation (Georgia Southern University CBAER, December 8, 2023) is unambiguous: "throughout the GMTC's active period, **no tax credits were awarded**. Despite receiving **six applications**… **none of these were approved**." All six lacked the GDEcD pre-certification letter and were denied. Had all six been approved, the lifetime total would have been **$561,000** — about 3.7% of a single year's cap, spread across five years. The credit ran 2018 through 2022 and was allowed to expire January 1, 2023. **This is the strongest available evidence that a statutory cap is not a measure of state support.** Georgia's own Tax Expenditure Reports show only "(m) — less than $1 million"; that notation was masking zero, not a small positive number. The evaluators blamed a $500,000 minimum spend well above peer states, a 15–20% rate against peers' 25–35%, and confusion created by overlapping the credit with the film credit.

17. **Louisiana's two music credits both closed to new applications on June 30, 2025, having barely been used.** Realized revenue loss, from the LDR Tax Exemption Budget 2025-2026:

| Program | FYE 6/2023 | FYE 6/2024 | FYE 6/2025 | Annual cap | Share of cap, FY25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sound Recording Investor (R.S. 47:6023), 18% | $47,229 | $22,642 | $60,460 | $2,160,000 | **2.8%** |
| Musical & Theatrical Production (R.S. 47:6034) | $758,797 | $852,752 | $2,143,446 | $10,000,000 | **21.4%** |

The sound recording credit ran at **1 to 3 percent of its cap** for three straight years. Statute, verbatim: "No credits shall be granted pursuant to the provisions of this Section for applications received on or after July 1, 2025."

18. **Louisiana tried to save the sound recording credit and the Governor vetoed it.** HB 653 (2025) would have raised the rate from 18% to 25% and extended applications through 2030. It passed the House **89–7** and the Senate **32–5** — and was **vetoed on June 20, 2025**. A textbook case of why reading statutes without checking veto history misleads.

19. **Neither Louisiana music credit has ever been evaluated, by design.** Louisiana's mandatory return-on-investment analysis (R.S. 47:1517.1) covers only incentives with revenue loss above $1 million. Both music programs fell below the threshold, so **no ROI ratio or jobs estimate has ever been published for either.** Confirmed by reading the LDR ROI report in full — the music programs are absent.

20. **New York's Broadway credit is an order of magnitude larger than anything in the South, and it ran out of money early.** N.Y. Tax Law § 24-c: **25% of qualified production expenditures, fully refundable**, up to **$3,000,000** per Level 1 production. The lifetime aggregate cap escalated $100M → $200M → $300M → $400M (FY2026 budget, May 2025) → **$550,000,000** (FY2027 budget, May 2026, now in statute). Credit period ends **September 30, 2027**; the statute is **repealed January 1, 2028**.

21. **NY: $308 million awarded to 150 productions between August 2022 and February 2026** — 129 Broadway and 21 Off-Broadway shows. **At least $179 million** of that went to shows that ran **fewer than 200 performances each**; 135 of the 150 had already closed. "KPOP" received $1.9M for 17 performances, over $110,000 per performance. This allocation list is **not published by ESD** — it exists publicly only because New York Focus obtained it via a FOIL request (reported 2026-03-19). The fund **ran dry roughly two years before its scheduled end**, prompting the January 2026 request for another $150M.

22. **NY: the profit-sharing clawback has never once been triggered.** Productions earning ongoing revenue at or above **200%** of ongoing costs must return up to **50%** of credits received to the New York State Council on the Arts cultural program fund. Per ESD, more than five years in, **no show has ever hit it.** Directly relevant if Texas ever considers a recapture provision.

23. **NY claimed-credit series (NYS Tax Expenditure Report, FY2027 edition):** $14.5M (TY2021), **$85.4M (TY2022)**, TY2023 incomplete, **$100.0M forecast for 2026**. Note these differ from allocated amounts — authorized, allocated, and claimed are three different numbers and the paper should never mix them in one column.

24. **Tennessee's "music incentive" is a film-scoring subsidy, and its music share is unknowable from public documents.** The TEC Scoring Incentive Program pays a **25% cash grant** on qualified Tennessee expenditures for producing **original scores for film, television, animation, commercials, gaming, and multimedia** — not record production, artist development, touring, or venues. **Music videos are expressly ineligible.** Minimum spend **$50,000 per project** in the Nashville metro, **$25,000** in the Knoxville, Chattanooga, and Memphis metros; capped at **$500,000 per company per year**. Administered by the Tennessee Entertainment Commission within TNECD; grants are discretionary and appropriation-limited, not an entitlement. *(Correction to the tasking: the guidelines cite the **Visual Content Modernization Act of 2018**, not the 2006 act — the 2006 law established the underlying fund. The T.C.A. section numbers were not verified; do not publish "§ 4-3-4801 et seq." without checking.)*

25. **Tennessee's film-and-music line has grown sharply but the recurring base has not.** Budget line **330.17 Film and Television Incentive Fund** (3 FTE) funds both the film/TV incentive and the scoring incentive, with **no music breakout**:

| Fiscal year | State appropriation | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| FY2023-24 | $1,794,500 | actual |
| FY2024-25 | $7,620,200 | actual |
| FY2025-26 | $10,686,500 | estimated |
| FY2026-27 | $28,687,100 | recommended |

Two cautions. FY2024-25 was *estimated* at $10,668,300 and came in at **$7,620,200 actual — 29% below estimate**, so treat estimates skeptically. And the FY2026-27 figure is a **$8,687,100 recurring base plus a one-time, non-recurring $20,000,000** described verbatim as funding "film production and music incentive grants." Presenting $28.7M as Tennessee's ongoing commitment would misstate it.

26. **New York's *upstate* program is small and underused.** Tax Law § 24-a, the Empire State Musical and Theatrical Production Credit (2015), carries an **$8 million annual cap**. In CY2024 it drew **7 applications and issued 3 credits totaling $2,142,537** against $8,570,147 in qualified costs — **27% of the cap**. This is the program ESD actually publishes an annual report for; the far larger Broadway credit has no equivalent public report.

27. **None of the peer-state credits does what Texas's does.** Every live-performance credit inventoried — Illinois, Louisiana, Maryland, Missouri, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Georgia — subsidizes **production companies mounting touring or Broadway-style shows**. Not one subsidizes an operating small venue. Texas's TMIR is a venue-operating subsidy and **has no clean analogue in the peer set**. Head-to-head dollar comparisons are misleading unless that difference is stated. Source: Georgia DOAA evaluation, Table 5 — which is a better cross-state inventory than anything NCSL currently publishes.
